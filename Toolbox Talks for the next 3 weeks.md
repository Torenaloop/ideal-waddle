## MOVEMENT MATTERS MAY: SUPPORTING MENTAL WELL-BEING THROUGH DAILY MOVEMENT

May is Mental Health Awareness Month, and “Movement Matters May” is a reminder that mental well-being is supported by small, everyday habits. In data center operations and office work, long periods of sitting, screen time, monitoring tasks, and high-focus work can make stress and fatigue feel heavier. Physical activity can help reduce short-term feelings of anxiety in adults and support thinking, learning, and judgment, even when movement is brief and simple. This is not about intense workouts; it is about building safe, realistic movement into the day.

**What Can Affect Mental Well-Being at Work?**

• Long periods of sitting during monitoring, meetings, or computer-based tasks  
• Repetitive work with limited movement or limited social interaction  
• Stress from deadlines, shift changes, alarms, tickets, or competing priorities  
• Skipping breaks because the day feels too busy  
• Feeling disconnected from coworkers or working through stress alone  
• Physical tension, headaches, irritability, low energy, or difficulty focusing

**Ways to Add Movement Into the Workday**

• Take short movement breaks when work conditions allow, even two to five minutes can help reset your focus.  
• Stand, stretch, or walk briefly between tasks, meetings, or handoffs.  
• Stretch the areas that tighten up during computer work: neck, shoulders, wrists, back, hips, and legs.  
• Use safe walking routes during breaks and avoid walking while distracted by phones or emails.  
• Pair movement with hydration by taking a short walk to refill water or step away from your screen.  
• Invite a coworker to take a quick walk or stretch break when appropriate. Connection matters too.  
• Listen to your body. If movement causes pain, dizziness, or unusual discomfort, stop and seek support.  
• Use available mental health resources when stress feels difficult to manage or starts affecting sleep, focus, mood, or relationships.

Small movement breaks can support both mental and physical well-being. A short walk, stretch, or reset moment can help reduce tension, improve focus, and make the workday feel more manageable. Taking care of your mind and body is part of working safely and supporting one another.

## ELECTRICAL SAFETY: SAFE POWER USE IN DATA CENTERS AND OFFICES

May is National Electrical Safety Month, making this a good time to refocus on safe power use in both data center and office environments. Electrical safety matters because power is central to our work, but damaged cords, overloaded outlets, improper temporary wiring, and unapproved equipment can create shock, burn, fire, and operational risks. The 2026 National Electrical Safety Month campaign highlights electrical safety awareness, including data center safety and reducing electrically related fires, injuries, and property loss. Good electrical habits protect people, equipment, and uptime.

**Common Electrical Hazards**

• Frayed, cracked, pinched, or damaged cords  
• Warm outlets, buzzing sounds, sparks, or burning smells  
• Overloaded outlets, power strips, or circuits  
• “Daisy chaining” power strips or extension cords together  
• Temporary cords being used as permanent wiring  
• Cords routed through doorways, under carpets, or across walkways  
• Liquids, leaks, or moisture near electrical equipment  
• Blocked electrical panels, disconnects, or emergency equipment  
• Unapproved chargers, adapters, or equipment brought from home  
• Unqualified employees opening panels, resetting equipment, or working on energized parts

**Safe Power Practices**

• Inspect cords, plugs, chargers, and portable equipment before use.  
• Remove damaged equipment from service and report it right away. OSHA guidance for cord-and-plug equipment includes visual inspection and removal of damaged items until they are made safe.  
• Plug equipment into approved outlets, PDUs, or power sources only.  
• Do not overload outlets or connect multiple power strips together.  
• Keep electrical panels, disconnects, and access areas clear at all times.  
• Keep liquids away from electrical equipment, workstations, and power sources.  
• Use extension cords only when approved and appropriate for the task.  
• Never ignore signs of electrical trouble, including heat, odor, flickering, tripping breakers, or unusual noise.  
• Only qualified personnel should perform electrical troubleshooting, testing, or repairs.  
• Stop and report the condition if something does not look or feel safe.

Electricity is essential to our work, but it must be respected. Safe electrical behavior starts with noticing small warning signs, using equipment correctly, and speaking up before a minor issue becomes a serious event.

## AIR QUALITY AWARENESS: PROTECTING HEALTH DURING SMOKE AND POOR AIR DAYS

Air Quality Awareness Week is May 4–8, 2026, and it is a timely reminder that outdoor air conditions can affect us at work, during commutes, and while performing outdoor tasks. EPA’s AirNow provides current air quality information, and EPA also points to the Fire and Smoke Map for wildfire smoke conditions. Even though data centers and offices are controlled indoor environments, employees may still be exposed during loading dock work, roof or yard activities, generator support, outdoor breaks, or travel between buildings. Poor air quality can irritate the eyes, throat, and lungs and may be more serious for people with asthma, heart conditions, lung conditions, or other sensitivities.

**What Can Affect Air Quality Around Work?**

• Wildfire smoke and fine particles  
• High ozone or smog days  
• Dust from construction, landscaping, or nearby traffic  
• Vehicle or equipment exhaust near doors, docks, or air intakes  
• Generator activity or testing areas  
• Propped exterior doors that allow smoke, odors, or dust indoors  
• Outdoor tasks during high heat, stagnant air, or poor air quality alerts

**Signs You May Be Affected**

• Burning or watery eyes  
• Scratchy throat, coughing, or congestion  
• Shortness of breath or chest tightness  
• Headache, fatigue, or dizziness  
• Worsening asthma or allergy symptoms  
• Unusual odors or visible haze around the site

**How to Reduce Exposure**

• Check local air quality before outdoor work, especially during wildfire season or poor air alerts.  
• Use AirNow or local emergency alerts to understand current conditions. EPA recommends AirNow for current air quality and the Fire and Smoke Map during fire events.  
• Limit outdoor exertion when air quality is poor and move breaks indoors when possible.  
• Keep exterior doors closed when smoke, dust, or exhaust is present.  
• Report smoke odors, unusual haze, or air quality concerns to the appropriate site contact.  
• Avoid idling vehicles near entrances, loading docks, or air intakes.  
• Follow site direction for HVAC settings, outdoor work adjustments, or respiratory protection.  
• Hydrate and take symptoms seriously. Move indoors and notify a supervisor if you feel unwell.  
• Look out for coworkers who may be more sensitive to poor air quality.

Air quality can change quickly, especially during wildfire smoke events or high ozone days. Staying aware, reducing outdoor exposure, keeping contaminants out of buildings, and reporting concerns early help protect both personal health and workplace comfort.
