## Question #1: _"What is the difference between fall protection versus fall restraint?"_
 
The primary difference between **fall protection** and **fall restraint** lies in their purpose and how they are designed to manage the risk of falling:  
**1. Fall Protection**

- **Purpose**: Protects a worker after they have fallen by stopping or arresting the fall to prevent serious injury.
- **Key Features**:
    - Involves systems like **personal fall arrest systems**, which include a **harness**, **lanyard**, and **anchor point**.
    - Allows the worker to access areas where a fall is possible but ensures the fall is stopped within a safe distance (no more than 6 feet of free fall is typically allowed).
    - **Examples**:
        - Using a full-body harness and shock-absorbing lanyard on a mobile elevated platform.
        - Anchoring to a lifeline when working on a roof without guardrails.
    - Requires additional safety planning for **rescue procedures** in case the worker becomes suspended.
 
**2. Fall Restraint**

- **Purpose**: Prevents a worker from reaching a fall hazard, effectively eliminating the risk of falling altogether.
- **Key Features**:
    - Uses a system to physically restrain or limit the worker's movement so they cannot get close to the fall hazard.
    - Usually involves a **harness** or **body belt**, a **restraint lanyard**, and an **anchor point**.
    - **Examples**:
        - A worker on a roof uses a short lanyard that prevents them from getting close to the roof edge.
        - A worker on a platform is tethered to an anchor point that limits their range of movement.
    - Does not require a rescue plan since the worker cannot fall.
 
**Key Differences:**

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Aspect**|**Fall Protection**|**Fall Restraint**|
|**Purpose**|Protects after a fall has occurred.|Prevents the worker from reaching the hazard.|
|**Use Case**|Where falls are possible but must be arrested.|Where falls can be completely prevented.|
|**System Components**|Full-body harness, shock-absorbing lanyard, self-retracting lifeline (SRL), anchor points.|Body belt or harness, fixed-length lanyard, anchor points.|
|**Rescue Plan**|Required (for suspension after fall arrest).|Not required.|
 
**When to Use Which?**

- **Fall Restraint**: Preferred when possible because it eliminates the fall risk entirely. For example, working near a roof edge with a lanyard that keeps the worker away from the edge.
- **Fall Protection**: Used when fall restraint isn’t feasible or practical, such as working on a scaffold or an aerial lift where a fall arrest system is needed to stop a potential fall.

## Question #2: _"If I have a contractor working on a mobile elevated platform, do they have to wear fall protection?"_
 
Yes, contractors working on a mobile elevated platform (MEWP) **must wear fall protection** as per the STACK AMER Mobile Elevated Platform Procedure and OSHA regulations. Here are the key requirements:  
**Fall Protection for MEWPs**

1. **Full Body Harness**:
    - Contractors must wear a full body harness.
    - The harness must be attached to the manufacturer’s engineered anchor point on the MEWP basket or platform.
2. **Restraint System Lanyard**:
    - A restraint system lanyard should be used to prevent the individual from being ejected from the platform or falling over the guardrails.
3. **Prohibited Practices**:
    - Standing on handrails, mid-rails, or top rails is strictly prohibited.
    - Self-retracting lanyards are not permitted for use in MEWPs unless specifically allowed by the manufacturer.
4. **Job Safety Analysis (JSA)**:
    - Fall protection requirements, including proper harness use, must be documented in the Job Safety Analysis (JSA) for the work activity.
5. **Training Requirements**:
    - Contractors must have completed appropriate training on fall protection systems as outlined in the Mobile Elevated Platform Procedure and Fall Protection Procedure【11†source】【15†source】.

**Additional MEWP Safety Considerations**

- Ensure a spotter is present when the MEWP is in motion or near hazards.
- Barricade the area below the MEWP to protect others from falling objects.
- Inspect the MEWP and all fall protection equipment before use.

## Question #3: _"Do I have to fill out a confined space permit if I have contractors entering an electrical vault?"_
 
Yes, you need to fill out a confined space permit if contractors are entering an electrical vault that qualifies as a **permit-required confined space**. Electrical vaults often fall into this category due to the following reasons:

1. **Hazardous Atmosphere**: Electrical vaults may accumulate gases like hydrogen from batteries or other harmful vapors, which could lead to oxygen deficiency or create a flammable atmosphere.
2. **Electrical Hazards**: The presence of live electrical equipment poses a significant risk, making it necessary to isolate the energy source (using Lockout/Tagout procedures) and ensure proper safety measures are in place.
3. **Configuration Hazards**: Vaults may have limited access points or other structural features that increase risks to entrants.
4. **Other Hazards**: Potentially high temperatures, poor ventilation, or other safety hazards.

**Steps to Take:**

1. **Evaluate the Vault**: Conduct a confined space hazard assessment using your facility's **Confined Space Assessment Form** to determine if it is a permit-required confined space.
2. **Fill Out the Permit**: If the vault is deemed permit-required, prepare a confined space permit, ensuring all hazards are documented, proper controls are in place, and the necessary atmospheric testing is completed before entry【10†source】.
3. **Coordinate with Contractors**:
    - Provide them with a copy of your facility's confined space policy.
    - Ensure contractors are trained and understand their roles and responsibilities under your confined space entry procedures.
    - Confirm the contractors’ procedures meet or exceed your internal safety requirements.

Remember to also verify that **emergency rescue resources** are on-site or readily available and that communication between the contractors, attendants, and supervisors is established. If you’re uncertain about specific hazards or procedures, consult your safety or operations manager.

## Question #4: _"When do I need to execute a confined space permit?"_
 
A confined space permit is required whenever a permit-required confined space is identified and entry is necessary. Permit-required confined spaces are spaces that meet the following conditions:

1. **Hazardous Atmosphere**: The space contains or has the potential to contain a hazardous atmosphere, such as oxygen deficiency, flammable gases, or toxic substances.
2. **Engulfment Hazards**: The space contains materials that could potentially engulf an entrant.
3. **Configuration Hazards**: The internal configuration could trap or asphyxiate an entrant due to inwardly converging walls or a floor that slopes downward and tapers to a smaller cross-section.
4. **Other Recognized Hazards**: The space contains any other serious safety or health hazard.

The confined space permit ensures:

- Pre-entry atmospheric testing is conducted.
- All hazards are identified, and control measures are in place.
- Entry supervisors authorize the entry, and attendants and authorized entrants are assigned.
- Emergency rescue plans and resources are available【10†source】.
