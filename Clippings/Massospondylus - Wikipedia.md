***==Massospondylus==*** ( [*MASS-oh-spon-di-lus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key") [^6]) is a [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus "Genus") of [sauropodomorph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauropodomorph "Sauropodomorph") dinosaur from the [Early Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Jurassic "Early Jurassic") of [southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa "Southern Africa"). It was described by [Richard Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Owen "Richard Owen") in 1854 from remains discovered in ==South Africa==, and is thus one of the first dinosaurs to have been named. The name *Massospondylus* means 'longer vertebra', alluding to what Owen identified as tail [vertebrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebra "Vertebra"); these vertebrae are now known to be [from the neck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cervical_vertebrae "Cervical vertebrae"). Although the original [fossils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil "Fossil") were destroyed in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") during a [bombing raid in World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz"), a plethora of specimens have since been assigned to the genus, making it one of the best-known sauropodomorphs from the Early Jurassic. The genus lived during the [Hettangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hettangian "Hettangian"), [Sinemurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinemurian "Sinemurian"), and [Pliensbachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliensbachian "Pliensbachian") ages, which lasted from ca. 201 to 184 [million years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annum "Annum"). Most fossils come from the upper [Elliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliot_Formation "Elliot Formation") and [Clarens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarens_Formation "Clarens Formation") formations of South Africa and Lesotho, but the genus is also found in the [Forest Sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Sandstone "Forest Sandstone") and the [Mpandi Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mpandi_Formation "Mpandi Formation") of Zimbabwe. Material from the US, India, and Argentina was previously assigned to the genus, but the US and Argentinian specimens are now assigned to their own genera (*[Sarahsaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarahsaurus "Sarahsaurus")* and *[Adeopapposaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adeopapposaurus "Adeopapposaurus")*). Because of their great abundance, *Massospondylus* fossils have been used to [date rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biostratigraphy "Biostratigraphy"), and a [biozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biozone "Biozone"), the *Massospondylus* Range Zone, is named after the genus.

Two species are considered valid: the [type species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_species "Type species") *M. carinatus*, as well as *M. kaalae*, which was named in 2009 and is known from a single skull. Six other species have been named during the past 150 years but are no longer recognised. Originally, *Massospondylus* and similar dinosaurs have been regarded as [theropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theropod "Theropod"), but are now classified as [basal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_\(phylogenetics\) "Basal (phylogenetics)") ("early diverging") members of Sauropodomorpha. This group also includes [sauropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauropod "Sauropod"). Within sauropodomorphs, *Massospondylus* is often classified in the family [Massospondylidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massospondylidae "Massospondylidae"). The genus was 4–6 metres (13–20 ft) long, with a long neck and tail, a small head, and a slender body. It is distinguished from related genera by the very elongated vertebrae of the front portion of the neck, amongst other features. Although *Massospondylus* was long depicted as [quadrupedal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruped "Quadruped") (four-legged), it is now considered to have been [bipedal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipedalism "Bipedalism") (two-legged).

It was probably a [herbivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbivore "Herbivore") (plant-eater), although some have speculated that basal sauropodomorphs may have been [omnivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnivore "Omnivore"). On each of its hands, it bore a sharp thumb claw that was probably used in feeding, possibly to uproot vegetation or to pull down branches while rearing up. Clutches of [eggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaur_egg "Dinosaur egg") have been found, some of which contained [embryos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryo "Embryo"); these are among the oldest eggs and embryos of an [amniote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amniote "Amniote") in the [fossil record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_record "Fossil record"). The eggshell was extremely thin, less than 0.1 mm (0.0039 in), unlike the much thicker eggshells in later dinosaurs. The embryos had proportionally longer arms than adults and a very large head, leading researchers to suggest that they were quadrupedal and shifted to a bipedal posture later during growth. Newer research instead suggested that *Massospondylus* was bipedal at all ages. Individuals accelerated or slowed down their growth depending on environmental factors such as food availability. The oldest known specimen was around 20 years of age.

## History of discovery

### Owen's 1854 description and loss of the type material

The first fossils of *Massospondylus* were described by [Richard Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Owen "Richard Owen") in 1854.[^7] The material, a collection of 56 bones or bone fragments, was found in 1853 or 1854 by the government surveyor [Joseph Millard Orpen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Millard_Orpen "Joseph Millard Orpen") and his brothers on a farm in the [Drakensberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drakensberg "Drakensberg") mountains near [Harrismith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrismith "Harrismith"), South Africa. Their father then donated the fossils to the [Hunterian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunterian_Museum,_London "Hunterian Museum, London") in London, of which Owen was curator.[^8] Owen named three new species from this material based on differences in their supposed tail vertebrae: *Massospondylus carinatus*, *Pachyspondylus orpenii*, and *Leptospondylus capensis*. The name *Massospondylus* (meaning 'longer vertebra') is derived from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") [μάσσων](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BC%CE%AC%CF%83%CF%83%CF%89%CE%BD "wikt:μάσσων") (massōn) 'longer' and [σπόνδυλος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%83%CF%80%CF%8C%CE%BD%CE%B4%CF%85%CE%BB%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:σπόνδυλος") (spondylos) 'vertebra'; Owen stated that he chose this name "because the vertebrae are proportionally longer than those of the extinct crocodile called *[Macrospondylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrospondylus "Macrospondylus")* ".[^7]<sup><span title="Page / location: 97">: 97</span> </sup> [^9] The specific name *carinatus* probably hints at the pronounced keel ([carina](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/carina "wiktionary:carina")) at the underside of the vertebrae. *Leptospondylus* means 'slender vertebra',[^1] while *Pachyspondylus* means 'thick vertebra'.[^2] [^7] [^8]<sup><span title="Page / location: 97–100">: 97–100</span> </sup> Among the bones in Orpen's collection were vertebrae from the neck, [back](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#dorsals "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), [hip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrum "Sacrum"), and [tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudal_vertebrae "Caudal vertebrae"); bones of the pelvis; the [humerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humerus "Humerus") (upper arm bone); and parts of the hindlimb including the [femur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femur "Femur") (thigh bone), [tibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibia "Tibia") (shin bone), and some foot bones. Orpen believed that more fossils would be found if the site were excavated.[^10] All these bones were probably found [disarticulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#disarticulated "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (not connected to each other), making it difficult to determine whether or not they belonged to the same species.[^9] Yet, Owen assigned most of the bones to either *Massospondylus*, *Pachyspondylus*, or *Leptospondylus*, which [Hans-Dieter Sues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Dieter_Sues "Hans-Dieter Sues") found to be somewhat arbitrary in 2004.[^11]<sup><span title="Page / location: 240">: 240</span> </sup> In the decades after Owen's publication, the three species were neglected by other scholars, which [Paul Barrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Barrett_\(palaeobiologist\) "Paul Barrett (palaeobiologist)") and Kimberley Chapelle speculated was due to Owen's "rather perfunctory descriptions", which lacked illustrations.[^8]<sup><span title="Page / location: 102">: 102</span></sup>

In 1888, [Richard Lydekker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lydekker "Richard Lydekker") studied the material and found *Leptospondylus* was likely a [synonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym_\(taxonomy\) "Synonym (taxonomy)") of *Massospondylus*, though he did not mention *Pachyspondylus*. Lydekker furthermore proposed that Owen's description was too incomplete for the name *Massospondylus carinatus* to be considered [valid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valid_name_\(zoology\) "Valid name (zoology)"), and that his own publication should instead be recognised as the source of the name. Consequently, he selected a neck vertebra and a toe bone as [type specimens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holotype "Holotype") (representative specimens on which a taxon is based). This proposal was mostly ignored by later authors, and Owen's description is valid according to the [International Code of Zoological Nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Code_of_Zoological_Nomenclature "International Code of Zoological Nomenclature") (ICZN).[^12] [^8] In 1895, [Harry Seeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Seeley "Harry Seeley") revised *Massospondylus* and illustrated many of the fossils for the first time.[^10] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 117">: 117</span> </sup> Seeley argued that the putative tail vertebrae of *Massospondylus* were actually neck vertebrae, and that the tail vertebrae of *Pachyspondylus orpenii* were probably those of *Massospondylus*. Therefore, he concluded that most of the fossils probably belonged to a single species and assigned them to *Massospondylus*, but noted that they represent at least three individuals.[^10] [^13] Another redescription of the Orpen specimens was published by [Friedrich von Huene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_von_Huene "Friedrich von Huene") in 1906, who assigned all material to *Massospondylus*.[^14] [^13] At the beginning of World War II, the basements of the Hunterian Museum were strengthened to protect specimens from German bombing raids, and several collections were moved to remote locations. The museum was damaged during several nearby bombings before it was directly hit by a bomb on the night between May 10 and 11, 1941, causing debris to fall into the dungeons and the building to be flooded due to heavy rain. Only 23 of 550 specimens in the museum's comparative anatomy collection survived. Many specimens that have been pivotal in the history of science were lost, as well as the entire Orpen collection, including *Massospondylus*, *Pachyspondylus*, and *Leptospondylus*, of which only illustrations and [plaster casts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casting "Casting") remain.[^8]

### Neotype specimen and M. kaalae

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b6/Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-1-full.png/250px-Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-1-full.png)

The neotype skull in left, right, and top views

By 1976, *Massospondylus* was the most widespread [sauropodomorph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauropodomorph "Sauropodomorph") known from southern Africa thanks to continued discoveries in South Africa, Lesotho, and Zimbabwe.[^8] In that year, James Kitching discovered clutches with eggs in a roadcut in [Golden Gate Highlands National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Gate_Highlands_National_Park "Golden Gate Highlands National Park"), a locality known as Rooidraai ('Red Bend').[^15] [^16] [^17] Already in 1961, a British-South African palaeontological expedition discovered the skeleton of a hatchling ([SAM-PK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iziko_South_African_Museum "Iziko South African Museum") -K413) at [Mafeteng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafeteng "Mafeteng"), Lesotho, although this specimen was first misidentified as the [varanopid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varanopidae "Varanopidae") *[Heleosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heleosaurus "Heleosaurus")*.[^17] In 1981, Michael Cooper published a comprehensive [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph#Biology "Monograph") on the Zimbabwean *Massospondylus* material, describing the entire skeleton apart from the skull, of which no material was available.[^18] He also discussed the [palaeobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeobiology "Palaeobiology") of the genus in detail for the first time. Cooper concluded that none of the other [gracile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gracile "Gracile") (slender-built) sauropodomorph species from South Africa differed substantially from *M. carinatus*, and consequently synonymised them all with the latter. A large number of specimens were assigned to *M. carinatus* as a result. Over the next two decades, new specimens of gracile sauropodomorphs from southern Africa were often assigned to *M. carinatus* by default and without much scrutiny because it was the only recognised species.[^8] The skull of *Massospondylus* was first described in detail in 1990 by Chris Gow and colleagues based on four well-preserved skulls housed at the [Evolutionary Studies Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_Studies_Institute "Evolutionary Studies Institute") [^3] in [Johannesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannesburg "Johannesburg").[^19] In 2004, [Hans-Dieter Sues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Dieter_Sues "Hans-Dieter Sues") and colleagues provided a more comprehensive description of the same four skulls, and proposed the first diagnosis of *M. carinatus* (the set of distinguishing features).[^8] [^11]

The five neck vertebrae on which *M. carinatus* was originally based (the [syntype series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntype "Syntype")) do not show distinguishing features and cannot serve as a basis for comparison. Consequently, Yates and Barrett proposed in 2010 to designate a different specimen, [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_Studies_Institute "Evolutionary Studies Institute") /1/4934, as the [neotype specimen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neotype "Neotype") (the new representative specimen).[^9] This specimen consists of an almost complete skeleton with skull housed at the Evolutionary Studies Institute. The skull was described in detail in 2018 by Chapelle and Jonah Choiniere, while Barrett and colleagues described the remainder of the skeleton in 2019.[^20] [^13] The specimen, nicknamed "Big Momma" although its sex is unknown, was found in March 1980 on a farm near [Clocolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clocolan "Clocolan"), South Africa, by Lucas Huma and [James Kitching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Kitching "James Kitching"). Other fossils were found on the same farm, including the [holotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holotype "Holotype") of the [turtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle "Turtle") *[Australochelys africanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australochelys_africanus "Australochelys africanus")* and the [cynodont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynodontia "Cynodontia") *[Pachygenelus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pachygenelus "Pachygenelus")*. "Big Momma" includes a nearly complete skull and large parts of an articulated skeleton. As of 2019, it is the largest and most complete *Massospondylus* specimen and probably the most complete [basal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_\(phylogenetics\) "Basal (phylogenetics)") (early diverging) sauropodomorph specimen discovered in Africa. Since 1990, it has been on public exhibit in the Evolutionary Studies Institute. To maintain the original positions of the bones as they had been found, the specimen was [prepared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_preparation "Fossil preparation") from above and below, but with most bones still partly encased in the original [rock matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(geology\) "Matrix (geology)"). However, the specimen has been divided into seven individual blocks, the heaviest of which is about 35 kg (77 lb) in weight.[^21] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 117–119">: 117–119</span> </sup> In the 2000s, the specimen was extensively restored after it became apparent that the fossils were deteriorating due to repeated handling. These conservation efforts included the filling of gaps and cracks in the bones, the application of a resin for hardening, and new support jackets to support the blocks.[^21]

Interactive 3D scan of the neotype skull

Besides the [type species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_species "Type species") *Massospondylus carinatus*, one additional species, *Massospondylus kaalae*, is currently recognised. *M. kaalae* is known from a single partial skull ([SAM-PK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iziko_South_African_Museum "Iziko South African Museum") -K1325) from the Upper Elliot Formation near [Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herschel,_South_Africa "Herschel, South Africa"), South Africa. This skull was collected in 1966 by Gow and others, but since then has remained undescribed in the [Iziko South African Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iziko_South_African_Museum "Iziko South African Museum") in Cape Town. In 2004, Barrett noted that this skull belonged to a new species, which he named *M. kaalae* in 2009. The species is named in honor of the museum's collections manager for the [Karoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karoo_Supergroup "Karoo Supergroup") vertebrate fossils, Sheena Kaal, for her assistance to numerous scientists who have studied specimens at the museum.[^13] [^22]

### Previously assigned material

#### Previously recognised species from southern Africa

In 1895, Seeley named the species *M. browni*, although its assignment to *Massospondylus* was only tentative. This species was based on two neck, two back, and three tail vertebrae as well as femora and toe bones that were discovered north of the [Witteberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witteberg "Witteberg") by Alfred Brown.[^10] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 174">: 174</span> </sup> In 1906, von Huene concluded that the vertebrae and femora of *M. browni* belonged to two separate species. He therefore restricted *M. browni* to just the femora, and moved the species into the genus *[Thecodontosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thecodontosaurus "Thecodontosaurus")*, as *T. browni*. He assigned the vertebrae as well as additional fossils stored in Vienna to another species, *[Hortalotarsus skirtopodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hortalotarsus_skirtopodus "Hortalotarsus skirtopodus")*, which he also moved into *Thecodontosaurus*, as *T. skirtopodus*.[^14]<sup><span title="Page / location: 145">: 145</span> </sup> In 1920, [Egbert Cornelis Nicolaas van Hoepen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egbert_Cornelis_Nicolaas_van_Hoepen "Egbert Cornelis Nicolaas van Hoepen") assigned a partial skeleton to *M. browni*, which was later assigned to *M. harriesi* and is now listed as a specimen of *M. carinatus*.[^8]<sup><span title="Page / location: 103">: 103</span> </sup> *M. browni* was considered an indeterminate sauropodomorph in a 2004 review by [Peter Galton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Galton "Peter Galton") and Paul Upchurch.[^23]

Two more species were described in the first half of the 20th century: *M. harriesi* and *M. schwarzi*. *M. harriesi* was named by [Robert Broom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Broom "Robert Broom") in 1911 based on parts of a forelimb and hind limb discovered near Fouriesburg. Broom suggested that the original material described by Owen included a second species besides *M. carinatus* which can possibly be assigned to his *M. harriesi*.[^24] Several additional specimens were assigned to *M. harriesi* in 1911 and 1924,[^8]<sup><span title="Page / location: 103">: 103</span> </sup> and in 1976, Galton and Michael Albert Cluver synonymised several other sauropodomorph species with it.[^25] *M. schwarzi* was named by [Sydney H. Haughton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_H._Haughton "Sydney H. Haughton") in 1924 based on an incomplete foot found near [Tlokoeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlokoeng "Tlokoeng") by Professor Schwarz.[^26] Both species were considered as indeterminate sauropodomorphs in the 2004 review.[^23]

#### Previous synonyms

Several other species from South Africa have originally been assigned to different genera, were later regarded as synonyms of *Massospondylus*, and are currently regarded as [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomen_dubium "Nomen dubium") (too incomplete to be classified). These include the above-mentioned *Leptospondylus capensis* and *Pachyspondylus orpenii*, as well as *[Hortalotarsus skirtopodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hortalotarsus_skirtopodus "Hortalotarsus skirtopodus")*, *[Aetonyx palustris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aetonyx_palustris "Aetonyx palustris")*, *[Gryponyx africanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gryponyx_africanus "Gryponyx africanus")*, *[Gyposaurus capensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyposaurus_capensis "Gyposaurus capensis")*, *[Gryponyx transvaalensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gryponyx_transvaalensis "Gryponyx transvaalensis")*, *[Thecodontosaurus minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thecodontosaurus_minor "Thecodontosaurus minor")*, *[Aristosaurus erectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristosaurus_erectus "Aristosaurus erectus")*, *[Dromicosaurus gracilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dromicosaurus_gracilis "Dromicosaurus gracilis")*, *[Thecodontosaurus dubius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thecodontosaurus_dubius "Thecodontosaurus dubius")*, and *[Gryponyx taylori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gryponyx_taylori "Gryponyx taylori")*.[^18] [^27] [^23] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 173–174">: 173–174</span></sup>

skeleton from the Clarens Formation near Ladybrand, South Africa. *Gryponyx taylori* is based on a fragmentary pelvis from the Upper Elliot Formation near Fouriesburg.[^26] [^25] [^18] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 173–174">: 173–174</span></sup>

In 1976, Galton and Cluver assigned *Aetonyx palustris*, *Gryponyx africanus*, *Gryponyx taylori*, *Dromicosaurus gracilis*, and *Thecodontosaurus dubius* to *M. harriesi*.[^25] Cooper, in 1981, instead considered all taxa as synonyms of *M. carinatus*. Galton, in a 1990 review, only listed *Aetonyx palustris*, *Gryponyx africanus*, *Gyposaurus capensis*, and *Gryponyx taylori* as synonyms of *M. carinatus*, while the 2004 review considers all species to be indeterminate sauropodomorphs rather than synonyms of *Massospondylus*.[^18] [^27] [^23] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 173–174">: 173–174</span></sup>

#### Other previously assigned specimens

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Massospondylus_hislopi_and_rawesi.png/250px-Massospondylus_hislopi_and_rawesi.png)

Teeth from India. Top: Tooth assigned to M. hislopi; bottom: type specimen of M. rawesi

During the 21st century, two additional massospondylid genera were identified in southern Africa, raising the question of whether all of the previous identifications of *Massospondylus* specimens were correct.[^13] The first of these genera, *[Ignavusaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignavusaurus "Ignavusaurus")*, was described in 2010 from a juvenile specimen. In 2011, Yates and colleagues considered it to be a probable synonym of *Massospondylus*,[^32] but [cladistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladistics "Cladistics") analyses led by Chapelle in 2018 and 2019 recovered it as a distinct taxon of massospondylid.[^20] [^33] The second genus, *[Ngwevu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngwevu "Ngwevu")*, was described in 2019 by Chapelle and colleagues based on a complete skull with a fragmentary skeleton that had been discovered in 1978 and was previously assigned to *M. carinatus*.[^33] In 2023, Chapelle and colleagues concluded that a humerus from the upper Elliot Formation, which also had been provisionally assigned to *Massospondylus*, belonged to a new sauropodomorph genus that was unusually small. This potential genus remains unnamed.[^34]

*Massospondylus* has been previously recognised from outside of Africa, namely from the [Lower Maleri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Maleri_Formation "Lower Maleri Formation") and [Takli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takli_Formation "Takli Formation") formations of India, the [Kayenta Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayenta_Formation "Kayenta Formation") of the US, and the [Cañón del Colorado Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca%C3%B1%C3%B3n_del_Colorado_Formation "Cañón del Colorado Formation") of Argentina. In 1890, Lydekker described the species *M. hislopi* and *M. rawesi* from fossils found in India. *M. hislopi* was based on a single fragmentary vertebra from the Lower Maleri Formation in [Andhra Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andhra_Pradesh "Andhra Pradesh"), while *M. rawesi* was based on a single tooth found by Mr. Rawes in the [Upper Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Cretaceous "Late Cretaceous") Takli Formation in [Maharashtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharashtra "Maharashtra").[^35] [^36] [^37] [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 173">: 173</span> </sup> In 1906, von Huene believed that both species were not dinosaurs.[^14] *M. hislopi* was listed as an indeterminate sauropodomorph in a 2004 review by Galton and Upchurch [^23] but *M. rawesi* is an indeterminate [theropod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theropoda "Theropoda").[^37] Other *Massospondylus* fossils from India were mentioned in 1987 by T.S. Kutty and colleagues but have instead been assigned to the family [Guaibasauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guaibasauridae "Guaibasauridae") in 2011.[^38] [^39] [^8] The material from the US, which consists of a nearly complete skull with skeleton described in 1985, was assigned to the new and closely related genus *[Sarahsaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarahsaurus "Sarahsaurus")* in 2010–2011.[^40] In 2009, the Argentinian material, which consists of several partial skeletons described in 1999, has likewise been recognised as a new genus, *[Adeopapposaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adeopapposaurus "Adeopapposaurus")*.[^41]

Cooper, in his 1981 study, suggested that the Chinese genera *[Yunnanosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yunnanosaurus "Yunnanosaurus")* and *[Lufengosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lufengosaurus "Lufengosaurus")* were synonyms of *Massospondylus*, which would have expanded its range to China. He classified *Lufengosaurus* as a separate species of *Massospondylus*, *M. huenei*. This suggestion was not followed by subsequent authors.[^18] [^8]

## Description

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/Massospondylus_scale_SW.svg/250px-Massospondylus_scale_SW.svg.png)

Size compared to a human

*Massospondylus* was a mid-sized sauropodomorph. In 2019, Barrett and colleagues estimated the particularly large neotype specimen at around 5 m (16 ft) in length.[^13] [Gregory S. Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_S._Paul "Gregory S. Paul"), in a popular book published in 2024, estimated *Massospondylus* to be 4.3 m (14 ft) in length.[^42] The body weight was estimated at 136.7 kg (301 lb) by Frank Seebacher in 2001, assuming a body length of 4 m (13 ft). Paul instead gave a body weight of 195 kg (430 lb).[^42] [^43] It was a typical basal sauropodomorph, with a slender body, a long neck, and a proportionally very small head. It had a slighter build than *[Plateosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateosaurus "Plateosaurus")*, an otherwise similar but larger dinosaur.[^23] Basal sauropodomorphs probably had [air sacs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_sac "Air sac") and possibly a bird-like [flow-through lung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung#Birds "Lung").[^44] In theropods and [sauropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauropod "Sauropod"), these air sacs invaded bones, in particular the vertebrae ([postcranial skeletal pneumaticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postcranial_skeletal_pneumaticity "Postcranial skeletal pneumaticity")). Although some basal sauropodomorphs such as *Plateosaurus* show incipient pneumaticity in their vertebrae, those of *Massospondylus* are apneumatic (lack any signs of pneumaticity).[^45]

### Skull

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Massospondylus_Skull_Steveoc_86.png/250px-Massospondylus_Skull_Steveoc_86.png)

Diagram of the skull of Massospondylus, showing the various skull openings

The skull of *Massospondylus* was small and approximately half the length of the [femur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#femur "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (thigh bone). Several openings in the skull, called [fenestrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenestra_\(anatomy\) "Fenestra (anatomy)"), reduced its weight and provided space for muscle attachment and sensory organs. At the front of the skull was the large [external naris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#external_naris "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (nostril), which was roughly half the size of the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#orbit "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (eye socket).[^23] The [posterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_\(anatomy\) "Posterior (anatomy)") (rear) margin of the external naris was semicircular, while the anterior (front) part of the opening was triangular. The orbit was circular and proportionally larger than that of *Plateosaurus*. The [antorbital fenestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antorbital_fenestra "Antorbital fenestra"), situated between the orbit and the external naris, was triangular and smaller than that of *Plateosaurus*.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 169–171">: 169–171</span> </sup> [^20]<sup><span title="Page / location: 8">: 8</span> </sup> At the rear of the skull were the two [temporal fenestrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_fenestrae "Temporal fenestrae"): the [lateral temporal fenestrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#lateral_temporal_fenestrae "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") immediately behind the orbit that was shaped like an inverted "T",[^11] and the supratemporal fenestra on the top of the skull. In side view, the temporal region (the region behind the orbits) was shorter than in *Plateosaurus*.[^46] A small fenestra, the [mandibular fenestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#mandibular_fenestra "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), also perforated the [mandible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandible "Mandible").[^23] Traditionally, the skull was thought to be wider and shorter than that of *Plateosaurus*, but these proportions may be the result of deformation during fossilisation.[^23] Some features of the skull are variable between individuals; for example, the thickness of the upper border of the orbits and the height of the posterior portion of the [maxilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#maxilla "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), the main tooth-bearing bone of the upper jaw. These differences may be due to [sexual dimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_dimorphism "Sexual dimorphism") [^19] or individual variation.[^47] A single [distinguishing feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autapomorphy "Autapomorphy") is found in the skull: the [basipterygoid processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#basipterygoid_processes "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), a pair of bony extensions that braces the [braincase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#braincase "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") against the [palate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#palate "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), form an angle of ca. 35°, whereas this angle is much larger in all other basal sauropodomorphs in which it can be measured.[^20]<sup><span title="Page / location: 55">: 55</span> </sup> [^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 171">: 171</span> </sup> The species *M. kaalae* differs from *M. carinatus* in the [morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_\(biology\) "Morphology (biology)") of the braincase; the proportionally longer premaxillary tooth row that accounted for more than 30% of the upper tooth row; and a better developed ridge on the upper edge of the [lacrimal bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#lacrimal_bone "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy").[^48]

![The same skull in top, bottom, front, and rear views](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-3-full.png/250px-Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-3-full.png)

3D reconstruction of the neotype skull in left and right side views (left) and top, bottom, front, and rear views (right)

Tooth count was variable between individuals and increased with skull size.[^19] The [premaxilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#premaxilla "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), the front bone of the upper jaw, showed the constant number of four teeth per side in all known skulls. In the maxilla, the tooth count varied from 14 to 22. There were 26 teeth on each side of the lower jaw in the largest known skull.[^11] [^19] The upper halves of the [tooth crowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tooth_crown "Tooth crown") had serrated edges, with three to eight [serrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#serrations "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") on each side.[^11] [^19] [^20]<sup><span title="Page / location: 70">: 70</span> </sup> Their height decreased from front to back in the upper jaw, but remained almost constant in the lower jaw.[^19] The rear edges of the teeth partially overlapped the front edges of the teeth behind them, forming a continuous cutting edge.[^19] [^20]<sup><span title="Page / location: 70">: 70</span> </sup> The teeth were [replaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#tooth_replacement "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") throughout life, as in other dinosaurs. This resulted in neighbouring teeth being of different heights, giving the tooth row a profile similar to a saw.[^19] The lack of pronounced [tooth wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tooth_wear "Tooth wear") suggests that tooth replacement was rapid;[^19] a 2013 study estimated that a tooth remained in use for only 17 to 30 days.[^49] The teeth varied in shape in different regions of the jaw; this [heterodonty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterodont "Heterodont") was more pronounced than that present in *Plateosaurus*.[^19] Teeth closer to the front of the snout had pointed tips and were slightly curved, unlike the posterior teeth that were [spatulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatula "Spatula") (with a broad, flat tip) and more symmetrical.[^23] The teeth were proportionally longer and slenderer than those of *Plateosaurus*.[^11]

### Postcranial skeleton

The [vertebral column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinal_column "Spinal column") was composed of nine cervical (neck) vertebrae, 13 dorsal (back) vertebrae, three sacral (hip) vertebrae, and at least 40 tail vertebrae.[^23] The neck was proportionally long, and the [centra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#centra "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (vertebral bodies) of the [anterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_terms_of_location "Anatomical terms of location") cervicals were more than seven times as long as they were tall, more elongated than in any other basal sauropodomorph and therefore a distinguishing feature. In the anterior cervicals, the neural spine (the bony keel that formed the top of each vertebra) was elongated, and its anterior end had a hook-like protuberance that constitutes another distinguishing feature.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 119, 124">: 119, 124</span></sup>

![Photograph of the bones on the left, and an interpretive line drawing with text labels on the right](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/Barrett-etal-2019-fig14-Massospondylus.png/250px-Barrett-etal-2019-fig14-Massospondylus.png)

Shoulder girdle and arm of the neotype specimen

The shoulder blade had an expanded upper end. This expansion was more symmetrical than in related genera, in which the [dorsal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_terms_of_location "Anatomical terms of location") (front) expansion was much larger than the [ventral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_terms_of_location "Anatomical terms of location") (rear) expansion, and is a distinguishing feature.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 119, 147">: 119, 147</span> </sup> In the hip, the [pubis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pubis_\(bone\) "Pubis (bone)") faced forward, as with most [saurischians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saurischia "Saurischia").[^23] The front end of the pubis was expanded in side view, but this expansion affected only its posterior margin, while the bone was flat anteriorly, which is a distinguishing feature.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 119, 165">: 119, 165</span> </sup> The forelimbs were only half the length of the hind limbs [^50] but quite powerful, as indicated by a broad flange on the upper portion of the [humerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#humerus "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") called the [deltopectoral crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#deltopectoral_crest "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), which provided attachment areas for a large arm musculature.[^51] This crest was longer than in related genera, extending for ca. 60% of the length of the bone, which is a distinguishing feature. The lower arm was proportionally short, with the [ulna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#ulna "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") measuring around 60% of the length of the humerus, which is another distinguishing feature.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 119, 152">: 119, 152</span> </sup> Like *Plateosaurus*, it had five [digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#digits "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") on each hand and foot. The hand was short and wide, with a large sickle-shaped thumb claw used for feeding or defence against predators. The thumb was the longest finger in the hand, while the fourth and fifth digits were tiny, giving the hands a lopsided look.[^51] [^18] The phalangeal formula of the hand, which states the number of finger bones from the thumb to the fifth digit, was 2-3-4-3-2.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 153">: 153</span> </sup> The foot also had a large claw on the first digit, which was curved and flattened laterally (side-to-side). The claws on the middle three digits instead were smaller, less curved, and flattened dorsoventrally (top to bottom). The fifth toe only had a single toe bone and no claw. The phalangeal formula of the foot was 2-3-4-5-1.[^18]<sup><span title="Page / location: 787">: 787</span></sup>

## Classification

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Massospondylus_BW.jpg/250px-Massospondylus_BW.jpg)

Life restoration of a bipedal adult Massospondylus carinatus

Described in 1854, *Massospondylus* was one of the first named dinosaurs from the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere "Southern Hemisphere").[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 114">: 114</span> </sup> Although Owen himself had named the group Dinosauria twelve years earlier in 1842, he did not recognise *Massospondylus* as a dinosaur. Instead, he attributed it to "large extinct [carnivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivorous "Carnivorous") Reptiles" of uncertain classification, noting similarities with [crocodilians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crocodilia "Crocodilia"), lizards, and dinosaurs.[^7]<sup><span title="Page / location: 97">: 97</span> </sup> [^10] In an 1860 book, Owen classified *Massospondylus* as a crocodilian but did not explain this decision.[^52] [^8] In 1888, Lydekker demonstrated that *Massospondylus* is a genus of dinosaur, and classified it in the family [Anchisauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchisauridae "Anchisauridae"), within [Theropoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theropoda "Theropoda").[^12] [^8] Seeley, in his 1895 revision, also found *Massospondylus* to be a theropod (a " [megalosaurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalosauria "Megalosauria") saurischian").[^10] [^8] Huene, in his 1906 redescription of the Orpen fossils, classified *Massospondylus* as a member of [Thecodontosauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thecodontosauridae "Thecodontosauridae"), together with *Thecodontosaurus* and *[Anchisaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchisaurus "Anchisaurus")*.[^14] In 1914, von Huene instead named a new family, [Massospondylidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massospondylidae "Massospondylidae"), to accommodate *M. carinatus*, *M. harriesi*, and *Aetonyx palustris*, but in 1932 returned to his former classification of *Massospondylus* within Thecodontosauridae.[^53] [^54] [^8]

In 1920, von Huene named the new group Prosauropoda to unite the early long-necked dinosaurs including *Massospondylus*. In 1932, he also named the new group Sauropodomorpha, which contained his Prosauropoda as well as Sauropoda, abandoning the traditional classification of *Massospondylus* and related genera within Theropoda.[^51] Yet, the relationships between Theropoda and Prosauropoda remained contested until 1965, when Alan J. Charig argued that Prosauropoda was more closely related to Sauropoda. Charig divided Prosauropoda into three families, following earlier studies: the small-sized Thecodontosauridae, which still included *Massospondylus*; the medium-sized [Plateosauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateosauridae "Plateosauridae"); and the large-sized [Melanorosauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanorosauridae "Melanorosauridae").[^55] [^51] This division of Prosauropoda into three families was generally upheld during the following decades, although several studies classified *Massospondylus* within the Plateosauridae or within another family, the Anchisauridae.[^56] [^51] In the 1990 edition of the encyclopedia *[The Dinosauria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dinosauria "The Dinosauria")*, Galton recognised additional prosauropod families including a resurrected Massospondylidae that contained *Massospondylus* as its only member.[^27] Massospondylidae has since been recognised by a majority of studies.[^56] The name "Prosauropoda" meanwhile fell into disuse as some prosauropods were closer to sauropods than to other prosauropods. Massospondylidae is instead classified within the group [Plateosauria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateosauria "Plateosauria"), which also includes the Plateosauridae, the [Riojasauridae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riojasauridae "Riojasauridae"), and, depending on the study, may or may not include sauropods.[^51] Adam Yates, in 2007, named the group [Massopoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massopoda "Massopoda") as a sub-group of Plateosauria that includes Massospondylidae and sauropods but not Plateosauridae.[^57]

Besides *Massospondylus* itself, Massospondylidae typically includes *Adeopapposaurus*, *Coloradisaurus*, *[Leyesaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyesaurus "Leyesaurus")*, and *Lufengosaurus*.[^8]<sup><span title="Page / location: 105">: 105</span> </sup> *[Glacialisaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacialisaurus "Glacialisaurus")* has also been consistently recovered as a member of Massospondylidae,[^58] [^59] and *[Pradhania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pradhania "Pradhania")*,[^60] *[Ignavusaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignavusaurus "Ignavusaurus")*,[^61] and *[Ngwevu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngwevu "Ngwevu")* [^33] might also be members. ## Palaeobiology

### Diet and feeding

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/Massospondylus_TD.png/250px-Massospondylus_TD.png)

Life restoration of Massospondylus carinatus

Basal sauropodomorphs such as *Massospondylus* may have been [herbivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbivory "Herbivory") or [omnivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnivore "Omnivore"). As recently as the 1980s, palaeontologists debated the possibility of [carnivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivore "Carnivore") in basal sauropodomorphs.[^18] [^63] However, the hypothesis of carnivorous sauropodomorphs has been discredited, and all recent studies favour a herbivorous or omnivorous lifestyle for these animals. In 2004, Galton and Upchurch found that cranial characteristics (such as jaw articulation) of most basal sauropodomorphs are closer to those of herbivorous reptiles than those of carnivorous ones, and the shape of the tooth [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(tooth\) "Crown (tooth)") is similar to those of modern herbivorous or omnivorous [iguanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iguana "Iguana"). The maximum width of the crown was greater than that of the root, resulting in a cutting edge similar to those of extant herbivorous or omnivorous reptiles.[^23] In 2000, Barrett proposed that basal sauropodomorphs supplemented their herbivorous diets with small prey or [carrion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrion "Carrion").[^64]

Like other basal sauropodomorphs, *Massospondylus* was an orthal feeder (its jaws made simple up-and-down movements).[^46] In their 1986 study, [Alfred Crompton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_W._Crompton "Alfred W. Crompton") and John Attridge suggested that *Massospondylus* and *Plateosaurus* had weak bites because their skulls were lightly constructed and had long tooth rows.[^4] [^65] However, the jaw joint was lowered with respect to the tooth row, which increased muscle leverage and therefore led to a stronger bite.[^46] In 2019, Ali Nabavizadeh suggested that *Massospondylus* and *Anchisaurus* had a stronger bite than other basal sauropodomorphs because of the more anterior position of the jaw joint, resulting in a more vertical action of the palatal muscles (*Musculus pterygoideus*).[^46]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-53-full.png/250px-Chapelle-etal-2018-fig-53-full.png)

CT scan of the teeth within the upper jaw of the neotype skull; replacement teeth are shown in orange.

As with other basal sauropodomorphs, it has been proposed that *Massospondylus* had fleshy cheeks, as there were few but large holes for blood vessels on the surfaces of the jaw bones, unlike the numerous small holes present on the jaws of cheekless reptiles. The cheeks would have prevented food from spilling out when *Massospondylus* ate.[^23] However, in the early 2020s, Nabavizadeh argued that cheeks in dinosaurs are speculative and unlikely.[^66] In 1986, Crompton and Attridge described skulls of *Massospondylus* as possessing pronounced [overbites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malocclusion "Malocclusion") and suggested the presence of a [horny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keratin "Keratin") beak on the tip of the lower jaw to make up the difference in length.[^65] However, the difference in length may be a misinterpretation and caused by crushing of the skull in a top–bottom plane, and more recent studies consider the possession of a beak as unlikely.[^11] [^67] [^68] Gow, in 1990, suggested that the articulation between the premaxilla and the maxilla allowed for some degree of [cranial kinesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranial_kinesis "Cranial kinesis") (movement between skull bones), and that the same was true for the articulation between the [quadrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#quadrate "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") and the [quadratojugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#quadratojugal "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy"), a condition known as [streptostyly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptostyly "Streptostyly").[^19] However, a later study found these articulations to be relatively rigid.[^68] [Gastroliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrolith "Gastrolith") (gizzard stones) have been found in association with three *Massospondylus* fossils from the Forest Sandstone in Zimbabwe.[^69] In one of these specimens, the preserved gastroliths made up ca. 1% of the body mass.[^51] Until recently, scientists believed that these stones functioned as a gastric mill to aid ingestion of plant material, compensating for their inability to chew, as is the case in many modern birds. However, Oliver Wings and Martin Sander showed in 2007 that the polished nature and the abundance of those stones precluded their use as an effective gastric mill in most non- [theropod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theropod "Theropod") dinosaurs, including *Massospondylus*.[^51] [^70]

### Posture, range of motion, and function of thumb claw

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Massospondylus_carinatus.001_-_Natural_History_Museum_of_London.JPG/250px-Massospondylus_carinatus.001_-_Natural_History_Museum_of_London.JPG)

Mounted *Massospondylus* skeleton cast (NHMUK PV R8171) at the [Natural History Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_Museum,_London "Natural History Museum, London"), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London"), showing an outdated quadrupedal pose [^71]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Massospondylus_Ultimate_Dinosaurs.jpg/250px-Massospondylus_Ultimate_Dinosaurs.jpg)

Mounted skeleton in modern, bipedal pose, [Royal Ontario Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Ontario_Museum "Royal Ontario Museum")

Although long assumed to have been [quadrupedal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruped "Quadruped") (four-legged), Matthew Bonnan and Phil Senter questioned this in 2007, arguing that the animal was restricted to [bipedal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipedal "Bipedal") (two-legged) gaits. Neither could the forelimb swing forward and behind in a fashion similar to the hindlimbs, nor could the hand be [pronated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronation "Pronation") (rotated so that the fingers face forwards when the forelimb is vertical). This inability to pronate the hand is also supported by findings of [articulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#articulated "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") (still-connected) arms that always show palmar surfaces facing each other. The study also ruled out the possibility of [knuckle-walking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knuckle-walking "Knuckle-walking") that would make effective locomotion possible without the need to pronate the hand. Bonnan and Senter suggested that some bipedal [trackways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_track "Fossil track") of the [ichnogenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichnogenus "Ichnogenus") *[Otozoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otozoum "Otozoum")* might have been produced by *Massospondylus* or similar bipedal sauropodomorphs.[^50]

It was often assumed that the large thumb claw of *Massospondylus* and other basal sauropodomorphs was used in defence against predators. In their 2007 study, Bonnan and Senter questioned this because the [humerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humerus "Humerus") could not swing forwards past the vertical. However, using the thumb claw for defence would have been possible if the animal reared up by using the [tail as a third "leg"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripod_stance "Tripod stance"). Although the hand could not reach the mouth, the thumb claw was probably used for feeding, such as for disrooting vegetation or, when rearing up, for pulling down branches.[^50] In 2025, Mooney and colleagues observed that the thumb claw is sickle-shaped in adult individuals but only slightly curved in the hatchling SAM-PK-K413 and relatively straight in one of the embryos. In the embryo, the thumb was already ossified while most other parts of the hands and feet were unossified, which might suggest that it had important functions already at early developmental stages.[^17]

Since the discovery of rudimentary and nonfunctional [clavicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clavicle "Clavicle") in [ceratopsians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceratopsia "Ceratopsia"), it was assumed that these shoulder bones were reduced in all dinosaurs that did not have true [furculae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furcula "Furcula").[^72] In 1987, [Robert Bakker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bakker "Robert Bakker") suggested that this would have allowed the [shoulder blades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scapula "Scapula") to swing with the forelimbs in quadrupedal dinosaurs, increasing their functional forelimb length.[^73] This would have reduced the discrepancy of length between fore- and hindlimbs in a quadrupedal *Massospondylus*. However, the *Massospondylus* specimen BP-1-5241 preserves well-developed clavicles that are joined in a furcula-like arrangement, showing that they acted as a clasp between the right and left shoulder blades, prohibiting any rotation of these bones. According to a 2005 study by Yates and Cecilio Vasconcelos, the specimen indicates that clavicle reduction is limited to the evolutionary line leading to the ceratopsians. It also indicates that the furcula of birds is derived from clavicles.[^72]

In 1981, Michael Cooper argued that the [zygapophyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zygapophyses "Zygapophyses") (projections that form joints between vertebrae) of the neck were inclined, impeding horizontal neck movements and forcing the animal to move its entire body instead.[^18] This was contradicted by Barrett and Upchurch in 2007, who noted that only the hindmost cervicals showed inclined zygapophyses, allowing sufficient horizontal movement of the neck as a whole.[^68]

### Growth

![Photograp of two skull casts, a large and a small, lying next to each other](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Massospondylus.jpeg/250px-Massospondylus.jpeg)

Photograp of two skull casts, a large and a small, lying next to each other

![3D reconstructions of the same specimens, with individual bones coloured and with their braincases showed separately](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Massospondylus_adult_juvenile_3D.png/250px-Massospondylus_adult_juvenile_3D.png)

3D reconstructions of the same specimens, with individual bones coloured and with their braincases showed separately

Skulls casts (left) and 3D reconstructions (right) of a juvenile specimen (top; BP/1/4376) and an adult specimen (bottom; [neotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neotype "Neotype") specimen). Bottom right: The reconstructed two braincases of both skulls.

Growth in dinosaurs can be reconstructed using [thin sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_section "Thin section") of bones that reveal growth rings, similar to the [growth rings in trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrochronology "Dendrochronology"). The first such study for *Massospondylus* was conducted in 1993 by [Anusuya Chinsamy-Turan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anusuya_Chinsamy-Turan "Anusuya Chinsamy-Turan") who, based on femora of different sizes, found that growth was cyclic, with one growth ring formed each year. Because an external fundamental system (tightly packed growth rings that indicate that growth had stopped) was absent, she suggested that *Massospondylus* had indeterminate growth (growth throughout life). This suggests that the [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaur_physiology "Dinosaur physiology") of *Massospondylus* was intermediate between [ectothermy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ectothermy "Ectothermy") ("cold-bloodedness") and [endothermy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermy "Endothermy") ("warm-bloodedness").[^74] In a 2001 study, [Gregory M. Erickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_M._Erickson "Gregory M. Erickson") and colleagues indicated that *Massospondylus* grew at a maximum rate of 34.6 kg per year and were still growing at around 15 [years of age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_determination_of_dinosaurs "Age determination of dinosaurs").[^75]

A 2005 study by Martin Sander and Nicole Klein indicated that the related *Plateosaurus* adjusted its growth according to environmental conditions: when food was plentiful or when the climate was favourable, *Plateosaurus* exhibited accelerated growth. Consequently, body size is only weakly correlated with age. This pattern of growth is called "developmental plasticity" and is common in ectothermic animals but uncommon in endothermic animals. According to Sander and Klein, developmental plasticity is absent in *Massospondylus*, which they found to grow along a specific growth trajectory with little variation in the growth rate and ultimate size of an individual.[^76] In 2022, Chapelle and colleagues analysed multiple different bones from 27 *Massospondylus* specimens and instead found substantial growth variation as in *Plateosaurus*, suggesting that developmental plasticity was widespread in basal sauropodomorphs. These authors also argue that *Massospondylus* had determinate growth, contradicting the 1993 study of Chinsamy that suggested that growth was indeterminate. Growth had stopped in the oldest analysed specimen, which was estimated at 20 years old.[^77]

### Eggs and embryos

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Massospondylus_egg_clutch_with_embryos_%28cast%29%2C_Golden_Gate_National_Park%2C_South_Africa%2C_Early_Jurassic_-_Royal_Ontario_Museum_-_DSC00145.JPG/250px-Massospondylus_egg_clutch_with_embryos_%28cast%29%2C_Golden_Gate_National_Park%2C_South_Africa%2C_Early_Jurassic_-_Royal_Ontario_Museum_-_DSC00145.JPG)

Cast of eggs and an embryo, Royal Ontario Museum

Although the Rooidraai nesting site was already discovered by Kitching in 1976, the embryos where not described before 2005.[^78] After additional excavations at the site between 2005 and 2012, Reisz and colleagues reported that 10 egg clutches from at least four fossiliferous horizons had been found, with up to 34 eggs per clutch, and that more clutches are likely present at the site. According to these authors, this indicates that this breeding site was [used repeatedly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_fidelity "Site fidelity") by [groups of animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_\(biology\) "Colony (biology)"), representing the oldest evidence of these behaviours. Sedimentary structures indicate that the breeding area was in the vicinity of a lake and has been repeatedly flooded. The arrangement of the eggs in tight rows might indicates that the eggs were pushed into this position by the adults, but [nest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nest "Nest") structures are not preserved.[^79]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Massospondylus_eggshell_SEM.png/250px-Massospondylus_eggshell_SEM.png)

Clutch with eggs (a), CT scan of egg with eggshell labelled "es" (b), and scanning electron microscope images of eggshell with arrows indicating the pores (c–d)

*Massospondylus* eggs are amongst the oldest known [amniote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amniote "Amniote") eggs from the fossil record, together with the roughly contemporaneous eggs of *Lufengosaurus* and *[Mussaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussaurus "Mussaurus")*. The eggshell consisted of a thick membrane covered by a layer of [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite "Calcite") that was very thin, less than 0.1 mm (0.0039 in), and had a rugged surface with few, unevenly distributed pores.[^80] [^79] [^17] Later dinosaurs had much thicker calcite layers, which must have evolved [independently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution "Convergent evolution") in the different groups. According to a 2019 study by Koen Stein and colleagues, the eggs were probably rigid rather than flexible despite their thin calcitic layers. This is because the calcite crystals interlocked with each other, and because the fossil eggshells retained their original shape.[^80] In contrast, a 2020 study by [Mark Norell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Norell "Mark Norell") and colleagues argued that the eggshell of early dinosaurs such as *Massospondylus* was soft, as the eggshell microstructure resembles that of the soft eggs of modern turtles.[^81] The thin shells allowed for gas exchange even in a low- [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") -rich environment, which indicates that the eggs were at least partly buried in the substrate.[^79]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/Massospondylus_embryo_3D_skull.png/250px-Massospondylus_embryo_3D_skull.png)

Digital reconstructions of embryonic Massospondylus skulls

The embryos are amongst the oldest dinosaur embryos ever found.[^78] [^5] [^16] The most complete embryonic skeleton is still [articulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossary_of_dinosaur_anatomy#articulated "Glossary of dinosaur anatomy") and belonged to an individual measuring about 8.1 cm (3.2 in) in [snout-vent length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snout-vent_length "Snout-vent length") (body length excluding the incompletely preserved tail).[^78] In 2010, Reisz and colleagues stated that five of the seven eggs contained embryonic remains, but in 2020, Chapelle and colleagues determined that such remains are present in only three of the eggs. The empty eggs could have resulted from various factors such as lack of [fertilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilisation "Fertilisation"), death of the embryo before the skeleton [ossified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossification "Ossification"), or loss during fossilisation.[^83] While the skeletal features of the embryos were similar to those of the adults, the body proportions were very dissimilar. The head was big with a short snout and very large orbits, whose diameter amounts to 39% of the entire skull length. The neck was short, in contrast to the very long neck in the adults. Girdle bones and tail vertebrae were relatively tiny. The forelimbs were of equal length to the hindlimbs and the head was proportionally very large.[^78]

In their 2005 study, Reisz and colleages suggested that the embryos were close to hatching based on their size in relation to the size of the eggs and their advanced ossification. Because the embryos lacked well-developed teeth and probably could not move around efficiently due to their body proportions, these authors proposed that they had no way of feeding themselves after hatching and possibly required [parental care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parental_care "Parental care").[^50] [^78] In their 2020 study, Chapelle and colleagues found that the *Massospondylus* embryos only completed about 60% of their [incubation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_incubation "Egg incubation") period, as modern animals show a comparable degree of ossification of various skull bones at this stage. These researchers also detected null-generation teeth – small and simple teeth without function that would be replaced by functional teeth before hatching – again suggesting an early developmental stage of the embryos.[^83] In 2025, Ethan Mooney and colleagues described an additional clutch of embryo-containing eggs that had already been collected by Kitching but not yet been [prepared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_preparation "Fossil preparation") and analysed. These embryos are slightly larger and more developed than the other embryos from the site, but their eggs had thinner shells, suggesting that eggshell was gradually resorbed as the embryo grew, as is the case with modern birds.[^17]

### Possible postural shift

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Massospondylus_baby_BW.jpg/250px-Massospondylus_baby_BW.jpg)

Restoration of a Massospondylus hatchling in a hypothetical quadrupedal posture

In 2005, Reisz and colleagues suggested that newly hatched *Massospondylus* must have been quadrupedal due to their long forelimbs and large heads, unlike the bipedal adults. The quadrupedality of the hatchings suggests that the quadrupedal posture of later [sauropods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauropod "Sauropod") may have evolved from retention of juvenile characteristics in adult animals, an evolutionary phenomenon known as [paedomorphosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paedomorphosis "Paedomorphosis").[^78] Hatchlings are known from a second sauropodomorph, *Mussaurus*; these remains resemble those of the embryonic *Massospondylus*, suggesting that quadrupedality was present in newly hatched *Mussaurus* and presumably other basal sauropodomorphs as well.[^50] [^16] In 2012, Reisz and colleagues suggested that tracks at the same site at which the embryos were discovered were produced by juvenile *Massospondylus*. These tracks include both pes (hindfoot) and manus (hand) tracks, with the manus tracks rotated outwards, suggesting a quadrupedal trackmaker with unpronated hands. Only the base of the thumb is impressed, suggesting that the enlarged thumb claw was held clear off the ground. The tracks vary in size but reach up to 15 mm (0.59 in) in length, larger than the estimated 7 mm (0.28 in) of a freshly hatched individual. Reisz and colleagues therefore speculated that *Massospondylus* hatchlings remained at the nest sites at least until they had doubled in size.[^79]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Massospondylus_hatchling_SAM-PK-K413.webp/250px-Massospondylus_hatchling_SAM-PK-K413.webp.png)

The hatchling SAM-PK -K413

In 2019, James Neenan and colleagues tested the postural shift hypothesis by analysing the [bony labyrinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bony_labyrinth "Bony labyrinth") of the [inner ear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_ear "Inner ear") based on [computed microtomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_microtomography "X-ray microtomography") scans of eight skulls covering the age spectrum from embryo to adult. The labyrinth houses the [sense of balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_of_balance "Sense of balance"), and one of its three canals (the lateral semicircular canal) is roughly horizontal when the head is in an alert posture in modern animals. Since Neenan and colleagues did not observe a change in orientation of this canal during growth, no support for a postural shift was found.[^84] In a 2020 study, Chapelle and colleagues also presented evidence against the postural shift hypothesis. These researchers argued that limb length ratios cannot reliably predict posture, and instead proposed an alternative method based on the minimum circumferences of the humeri and femora. Although the posture of the analysed *Massospondylus* embryo was found to be ambiguous, the hatchling ([SAM-PK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iziko_South_African_Museum "Iziko South African Museum") -K413) was found to be bipedal. This hatchling would have been the size of a newly hatched individual, suggesting that *Massospondylus* was bipedal at all ages. *Mussaurus*, in contrast, was confirmed to show a postural shift from juvenile to adult.[^85] In their 2025 study, Mooney and colleagues argued that the humerus shaft of the hatchling is incompletely preserved, so that posture cannot be reliably estimated based on minimum shaft circumferences for this key individual. These authors estimated that the forelimb is 71% of the hindlimb length in a smaller embryo, 69% in their newly described larger embryo, 58% in the smallest hatchling, and 44% in the neotype specimen, the largest known individual. These estimates suggest that the forelimb shortened during growth, which supports the postural shift hypothesis.[^17]

## Palaeoecology

### Distribution and abundance

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/20/Stormberg_Group_stratigraphy.png/250px-Stormberg_Group_stratigraphy.png)

Stratigraphy of the Stormberg Group of South Africa and Lesotho. Massospondylus is found in the upper Elliot and Clarens formations.

Most *Massospondylus* remains have been found in the upper [Elliot Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliot_Formation "Elliot Formation") and the [Clarens Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarens_Formation "Clarens Formation") of South Africa and Lesotho, which are part of the [Stormberg Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormberg_Group "Stormberg Group") within the [Karoo Supergroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karoo_Supergroup "Karoo Supergroup"). These [formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_formation "Geological formation") were deposited during the [Hettangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hettangian "Hettangian"), [Sinemurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinemurian "Sinemurian"), and [Pliensbachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliensbachian "Pliensbachian") ages of the [Early Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Jurassic "Early Jurassic"), ca. 201–184 [million years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annum "Annum").[^86] *Massospondylus* has also been found in the [Forest Sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Sandstone "Forest Sandstone") and the [Mpandi Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mpandi_Formation "Mpandi Formation") of Zimbabwe, which are thought to be contemporaneous with the upper Elliot and Clarens formations.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 114">: 114</span></sup>

*Massospondylus* is known from numerous almost complete skeletons, including at least 13 reasonably complete skulls and thousands of isolated bones and fragments.[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 114">: 114</span> </sup> [^20]<sup><span title="Page / location: 1">: 1</span> </sup> This wealth of material makes *Massospondylus* one of the best-known sauropodomorphs from the Early Jurassic. Because of its great abundance, it is used as the defining taxon of a [biozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biozone "Biozone"), the *Massospondylus* Range Zone, which is the uppermost such zone in the Karoo Supergroup. The presence of its fossils has been used to [determine the relative ages of sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biostratigraphy "Biostratigraphy").[^13]<sup><span title="Page / location: 114">: 114</span> </sup> [^9]

### Palaeoenvironment

During the deposition of the Stormberg Group, the climate became gradually drier. The oldest part of the group (the [Molteno Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molteno_Formation "Molteno Formation")) consists of [sandstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone "Sandstone") that were [deposited by rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluvial_sediment_processes "Fluvial sediment processes") and show evidence of heavy rainfall. As the Elliot Formation was deposited, the climate became [semi-arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate "Semi-arid climate") and the environment was characterised by short-lived rivers and lakes that deposited sandstones and [mudstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudstone "Mudstone"). During deposition of the upper Elliot Formation, [aeolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_processes "Aeolian processes") (wind) erosion and sedimentation created depressions in which [playa lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playa_lake "Playa lake") formed. The overlying Clarens Formation consists mostly of aeolian sandstones deposited by dunes in a desert environment.[^87]

The [faunas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna "Fauna") and [floras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora "Flora") of the Early Jurassic were similar worldwide, with [conifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinophyta "Pinophyta") adapted for hot weather becoming the common plants;[^88] [basal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_\(phylogenetics\) "Basal (phylogenetics)") sauropodomorphs and theropods were the main constituents of a worldwide dinosaur fauna.[^89] *Massospondylus* was a contemporary of [temnospondyli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temnospondyli "Temnospondyli"); turtles; a [sphenodont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphenodontia "Sphenodontia"); early [crocodylomorphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crocodylomorpha "Crocodylomorpha"); [cynodonts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynodont "Cynodont"); and early [mammaliforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammaliformes "Mammaliformes").[^90] Predatory dinosaurs included the small theropod *[Megapnosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megapnosaurus "Megapnosaurus")* as well as the 6-metre-long [^91] theropod *[Dracovenator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracovenator "Dracovenator")*, which might have preyed on the sauropodomorphs.[^92] Several genera of early [ornithischians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornithischia "Ornithischia") are known, such as *[Lesothosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesothosaurus "Lesothosaurus")* and the [heterodontosaurids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterodontosauridae "Heterodontosauridae") *[Abrictosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrictosaurus "Abrictosaurus")*, *[Heterodontosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterodontosaurus "Heterodontosaurus")*, *[Lycorhinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycorhinus "Lycorhinus")* and *[Pegomastax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pegomastax "Pegomastax")*.[^93] [^90] [^94] Until recently, *M. carinatus* was regarded as the only known sauropodomorph from the upper Elliot and Clarens formations.[^13] However, newer finds revealed a diverse contemporary sauropodomorph fauna with at least seven additional species from the upper Elliot Formation, including *[Aardonyx celestae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aardonyx_celestae "Aardonyx celestae")*, *[Antetonitrus ingenipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antetonitrus_ingenipes "Antetonitrus ingenipes")*, *[Ignavusaurus rachelis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignavusaurus_rachelis "Ignavusaurus rachelis")*, *[Arcusaurus pereirabdalorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcusaurus_pereirabdalorum "Arcusaurus pereirabdalorum")*, *[Pulanesaura eocollum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulanesaura_eocollum "Pulanesaura eocollum")*, *[Ngwevu intloko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngwevu_intloko "Ngwevu intloko")*, as well as a second species of *Massospondylus*, *M. kaalae*.[^13]
