**Sinn Féin** ([/ ʃ ɪ n ˈ f eɪ n /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*shin FAYN*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key");[^14] Irish: [\[ˌʃɪn̠ʲ ˈfʲeːnʲ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Irish "Help:IPA/Irish") ; lit. '\[We\] Ourselves') [^15] is an [Irish republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_republican "Irish republican") [^16] and [democratic socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_socialist "Democratic socialist") [^17] political party active in [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland "Ireland"), including both the [Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland "Republic of Ireland") and [Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland "Northern Ireland").

The [original Sinn Féin organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Sinn_F%C3%A9in "History of Sinn Féin") was founded in 1905 by [Arthur Griffith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Griffith "Arthur Griffith"). Its members founded the revolutionary [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic "Irish Republic") and its parliament, the [First Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_D%C3%A1il "1st Dáil"), and many of them were active in the [Irish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence "Irish War of Independence"), during which the party was associated with the [Irish Republican Army (1919–1922)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1919%E2%80%931922\) "Irish Republican Army (1919–1922)"). The party split before the [Irish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War "Irish Civil War") and again in its aftermath, giving rise to the two traditionally dominant parties of Irish politics: [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il "Fianna Fáil"), and [Cumann na nGaedheal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumann_na_nGaedheal "Cumann na nGaedheal") (which merged with smaller groups to form [Fine Gael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_Gael "Fine Gael")). For several decades the remaining Sinn Féin organisation was small and often without parliamentary representation. It continued its association with the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1922%E2%80%931969\) "Irish Republican Army (1922–1969)"). Another split in 1970 at the start of [the Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles "The Troubles") led to the modern Sinn Féin party, with the other faction eventually becoming the [Workers' Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers%27_Party_\(Ireland\) "Workers' Party (Ireland)").

During the [Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles "The Troubles"), Sinn Féin was associated with the [Provisional Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army "Provisional Irish Republican Army").[^18] For most of that conflict, it was affected by [broadcasting bans in the Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_31_of_the_Broadcasting_Act "Section 31 of the Broadcasting Act") and [British media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988%E2%80%931994_British_broadcasting_voice_restrictions "1988–1994 British broadcasting voice restrictions"). Although the party sat on local councils, it maintained a policy of [abstentionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstentionism "Abstentionism") for the British [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom "House of Commons of the United Kingdom") and the Irish [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann "Dáil Éireann"), standing for election to those legislatures but pledging not to take their seats if elected. After [Gerry Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Adams "Gerry Adams") became party leader in 1983, electoral politics were prioritised increasingly. In 1986, the party dropped its abstentionist policy for the Dáil; some members formed [Republican Sinn Féin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Sinn_F%C3%A9in "Republican Sinn Féin") in protest. In the 1990s, Sinn Féin—under the leadership of Adams and [Martin McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McGuinness "Martin McGuinness") —was involved in the [Northern Ireland peace process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_peace_process "Northern Ireland peace process"). This led to the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement "Good Friday Agreement") and created the [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly "Northern Ireland Assembly"), and saw Sinn Féin become part of the [power-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consociationalism "Consociationalism") [Northern Ireland Executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive "Northern Ireland Executive"). In 2006, it co-signed the [St Andrews Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrews_Agreement "St Andrews Agreement") and agreed to support the [Police Service of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Service_of_Northern_Ireland "Police Service of Northern Ireland").

Sinn Féin is the largest party in the Northern Ireland Assembly, having won the largest share of first-preference votes and the most seats in the [2022 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Northern_Ireland_Assembly_election "2022 Northern Ireland Assembly election"), the first time an [Irish nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalist "Irish nationalist") party has done so.[^19] [^20] Since 2024, [Michelle O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_O%27Neill "Michelle O'Neill") has served as the first ever Irish nationalist [First Minister of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Minister_of_Northern_Ireland "First Minister of Northern Ireland").[^21] From 2007 to 2022, Sinn Féin was the second-largest party in the Assembly, after the [Democratic Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Unionist_Party "Democratic Unionist Party") (DUP), and its nominees served as [Deputy First Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Minister_and_Deputy_First_Minister_of_Northern_Ireland "First Minister and Deputy First Minister of Northern Ireland") in the Northern Ireland Executive.

In the House of Commons of the United Kingdom, Sinn Féin has held seven of Northern Ireland's seats since the [2024 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election "2024 United Kingdom general election"); it continues its policy of abstentionism at Westminster. In [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann "Dáil Éireann") it is the main opposition, having won the second largest number of seats in the [2024 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Irish_general_election "2024 Irish general election"). The current [president of Sinn Féin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Sinn_F%C3%A9in "President of Sinn Féin") is [Mary Lou McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Lou_McDonald "Mary Lou McDonald"), who succeeded Gerry Adams in 2018.

## Name

The phrase "Sinn Féin" is [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language "Irish language") for "Ourselves" or "We Ourselves",[^22] [^23] although it is frequently mistranslated as "ourselves alone" (from " *[Sinn Féin Amháin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinn_F%C3%A9in_\(slogan\) "Sinn Féin (slogan)")* ", an early-20th-century slogan).[^24] The name is an assertion of Irish national sovereignty and self-determination, i.e., the [Irish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_people "Irish people") governing themselves, rather than being part of a political union with Great Britain under the [Westminster Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom "Parliament of the United Kingdom").

A split in January 1970, mirroring a split in the IRA, led to the emergence of two groups calling themselves Sinn Féin. The majority group, under the continued leadership of [Tomás Mac Giolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_Mac_Giolla "Tomás Mac Giolla"), became known as *Official Sinn Féin* or *Sinn Féin (Gardiner Place)*. The minority group, led by [Ruairí Ó Brádaigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruair%C3%AD_%C3%93_Br%C3%A1daigh "Ruairí Ó Brádaigh"), became known as *Provisional Sinn Féin* or *Sinn Féin (Kevin Street)*. *Official Sinn Féin* changed its name to *Sinn Féin-The Workers' Party* in 1977, and in 1982 it changed its name to *[The Workers' Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers%27_Party_\(Ireland\) "Workers' Party (Ireland)")*.[^25] As the "Official" group had dropped all mention of Sinn Féin from its name in 1982, the term "Provisional Sinn Féin" fell out of use, and in 1987 *Provisional Sinn Féin* registered as a political party in the Republic of Ireland under the name *Sinn Féin*.[^26]

Sinn Féin members have been referred to colloquially as "Shinners", a term intended as a [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative "Pejorative").[^27] [^28]

## History

### 1905–1922

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Arthur_Griffith_%281871-1922%29.jpg/250px-Arthur_Griffith_%281871-1922%29.jpg)

Arthur Griffith is credited as the main founder of the party

Sinn Féin was founded on 28 November 1905, when, at the first annual Convention of the National Council, [Arthur Griffith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Griffith "Arthur Griffith") outlined the Sinn Féin policy, "to establish in Ireland's capital a national legislature endowed with the moral authority of the Irish nation".[^23] Its initial political platform was both [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism "Conservatism") and [monarchist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchism "Monarchism"), advocating for an Anglo-Irish [dual monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_monarchy "Dual monarchy") unified with the British [Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown "The Crown") (inspired by the [Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Compromise_of_1867 "Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867")).[^29] [^30] The party contested the [1908 North Leitrim by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1908_North_Leitrim_by-election "1908 North Leitrim by-election"), where it secured 27% of the vote.[^31] Thereafter, both support and membership fell. At its 1910 *ard fheis* (party conference) attendance was poor, and there was difficulty finding members willing to take seats on the executive.[^32]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/McGuinness-_1917_election.jpg/250px-McGuinness-_1917_election.jpg)

The campaign car of Joseph McGuinness, who won the 1917 South Longford by-election whilst imprisoned. He was one of the first Sinn Féin MPs to be elected.

In 1914, Sinn Féin members, including Griffith, joined the anti-Redmond [Irish Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Volunteers "Irish Volunteers"), which was referred to by [Redmondites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Redmond "John Redmond") and others as the "Sinn Féin Volunteers". Although Griffith himself did not take part in the [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising "Easter Rising") of 1916, many Sinn Féin members who were members of the Volunteers and the [Irish Republican Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Brotherhood "Irish Republican Brotherhood") did. Government and newspapers dubbed the Rising "the Sinn Féin Rising".[^33] After the Rising, [republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_republicanism "Irish republicanism") came together under the banner of Sinn Féin, and at the 1917 *ard fheis* the party committed itself for the first time to the establishment of an [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic "Irish Republic"). In the [1918 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Ireland "1918 United Kingdom general election in Ireland"), Sinn Féin won 73 of Ireland's 105 seats, and in January 1919, its MPs assembled in Dublin and proclaimed themselves [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_D%C3%A1il "1st Dáil"), the parliament of Ireland. Sinn Féin candidate [Constance Markievicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constance_Markievicz "Constance Markievicz") became the first woman elected to the [United Kingdom House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom "House of Commons of the United Kingdom"). However, in line with Sinn Féin [abstentionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstentionist "Abstentionist") policy, she did not take her seat in the House of Commons.[^34]

The party supported the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1919%E2%80%931922\) "Irish Republican Army (1919–1922)") during the [War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence "Irish War of Independence"), and members of the Dáil government negotiated the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty "Anglo-Irish Treaty") with the British government in 1921. In the Dáil debates that followed, the party divided on the Treaty. The pro-Treaty and anti-Treaty components (led by [Michael Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Collins_\(Irish_leader\) "Michael Collins (Irish leader)") and [Éamon de Valera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89amon_de_Valera "Éamon de Valera") respectively) managed to agree on a "Coalition Panel" of Sinn Féin candidates to stand in the [1922 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_Irish_general_election "1922 Irish general election").[^35] After the election, anti-Treaty members walked out of the Dáil, and pro- and anti-Treaty members took opposite sides in the ensuing [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War "Irish Civil War").[^36]

### 1923–1970

Pro-Treaty Dáil deputies and other Treaty supporters formed a new party, [Cumann na nGaedheal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumann_na_nGaedheal "Cumann na nGaedheal"), on 27 April 1923 at a meeting in Dublin, where delegates agreed on a constitution and political programme.[^37] Cumann na nGaedheal went on to govern the new [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State "Irish Free State") for nine years (it merged with two other organisations to form [Fine Gael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_Gael "Fine Gael") in 1933).[^38] Anti-Treaty Sinn Féin members continued to boycott the Dáil. At a special *Ard Fheis* in March 1926, de Valera proposed that elected members be allowed to take their seats in the Dáil if and when the controversial [Oath of Allegiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_\(Ireland\) "Oath of Allegiance (Ireland)") was removed. When his motion was defeated, de Valera resigned from Sinn Féin; on 16 May 1926, he founded his own party, [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il "Fianna Fáil"), which was dedicated to republicanising the Free State from within its political structures. He took most Sinn Féin [Teachtaí Dála](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachta%C3%AD_D%C3%A1la "Teachtaí Dála") (TDs) with him.[^39] De Valera's resignation meant also the loss of financial support from America.[^40] The rump Sinn Féin party could field no more than fifteen candidates,[^41] and won only five seats in the [June 1927 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_1927_Irish_general_election "June 1927 Irish general election"), a decline in support not seen since before 1916.[^42] [^43] Vice-president and *[de facto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_facto "De facto")* leader [Mary MacSwiney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_MacSwiney "Mary MacSwiney") announced that the party simply did not have the funds to contest [the second election called that year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_1927_Irish_general_election "September 1927 Irish general election"), declaring "no true Irish citizen can vote for any of the other parties".[^43] Fianna Fáil came to power at the [1932 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1932_Irish_general_election "1932 Irish general election") (to begin what would be an unbroken 16-year spell in government) and went on to long dominate politics in the independent Irish state.

An attempt in the 1940s to access funds that had been put in the care of the [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_\(Ireland\) "High Court (Ireland)") led to the [Sinn Féin Funds case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinn_F%C3%A9in_Funds_case "Sinn Féin Funds case"), which the party lost and in which the judge ruled that it was not the legal successor to the Sinn Féin of 1917.[^44]

By the late 1940s, two decades removed from the Fianna Fáil split and now the Sinn Féin funds lost, the party was little more than a husk. The emergence of a popular new republican party, led by former IRA members, in [Clann na Poblachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clann_na_Poblachta "Clann na Poblachta"), threatened to void any remaining purpose Sinn Féin had left. However, it was around this same time that the IRA leadership once again sought to have a political arm (the IRA and Sinn Féin had effectively no formal ties following the civil war).[^45] Following an IRA army convention in 1948, IRA members were instructed to join Sinn Féin en masse and by 1950 they had successfully taken total control of the party, with IRA army council member [Paddy McLogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_McLogan "Paddy McLogan") named as the new president of the party. As part of this rapprochement, it was later made clear by the army council that the IRA would dictate to Sinn Féin, and not the other way around.[^46] [^47] [^48]

At the [1955 United Kingdom general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1955_United_Kingdom_general_election "1955 United Kingdom general election"), two Sinn Féin candidates were elected to Westminster, and likewise, four members of Sinn Féin were elected to Leinster House in the [1957 Irish general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1957_Irish_general_election "1957 Irish general election"). In December 1956, at the beginning of the IRA's [Border Campaign (Operation Harvest)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_campaign_\(Irish_Republican_Army\) "Border campaign (Irish Republican Army)"), the Northern Ireland Government banned Sinn Féin under the [Special Powers Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Authorities_\(Special_Powers\)_Act_\(Northern_Ireland\)_1922 "Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act (Northern Ireland) 1922"); it would remain banned until 1974.[^49] By the end of the Border campaign five years later, the party had once again lost all national representation.[^50] Through the 1960s, some leading figures in the movement, such as [Cathal Goulding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathal_Goulding "Cathal Goulding"), [Seán Garland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_Garland "Seán Garland"), [Billy McMillen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_McMillen "Billy McMillen") and [Tomás Mac Giolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_Mac_Giolla "Tomás Mac Giolla"), moved steadily to the left, even to [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism"), as a result of their own reading and thinking and contacts with the Irish and international left. This angered more traditional republicans, who wanted to stick to the national question and armed struggle.[^51] The Garland Commission was set up in 1967, to investigate the possibility of ending abstentionism. Its report angered the already disaffected traditional republican element within the party, notably [Seán Mac Stíofáin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_Mac_St%C3%ADof%C3%A1in "Seán Mac Stíofáin") and Ruairí Ó Brádaigh, who viewed such a policy as treason against the Irish Republic.[^52]

### 1970–1975

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Ruair%C3%AD_%C3%93_Br%C3%A1daigh_2004.jpg/250px-Ruair%C3%AD_%C3%93_Br%C3%A1daigh_2004.jpg)

Ruairí Ó Brádaigh (pictured in 2004) was the president of Provisional Sinn Féin from 1970 until 1983.

Sinn Féin split in two at the beginning of 1970. On 11 January, the proposal to end abstentionism and take seats, if elected, in the Dáil, the [Parliament of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Northern_Ireland "Parliament of Northern Ireland") and the Parliament of the United Kingdom was put before the members at the party's *Ard Fheis*.[^53] A similar motion had been adopted at an IRA convention the previous month, leading to the formation of a Provisional Army Council by Mac Stíofáin and other members opposed to the leadership. When the motion was put to the *Ard Fheis*, it failed to achieve the necessary two-thirds majority. The Executive attempted to circumvent this by introducing a motion in support of IRA policy, at which point the dissenting delegates walked out of the meeting.[^54] These members reconvened at Kevin Barry Hall in [Parnell Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnell_Square "Parnell Square"), where they appointed a Caretaker Executive with Ruairí Ó Brádaigh as chairman.[^55] The Caretaker Executive's first act was to pass a [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_\(debate\) "Resolution (debate)") pledging allegiance to the 32-county Irish Republic and the Provisional Army Council.[^56] It also declared itself opposed to the ending of abstentionism, the drift towards "extreme forms of socialism", the failure of the leadership to defend the nationalist people of Belfast during the [1969 Northern Ireland riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_Northern_Ireland_riots "1969 Northern Ireland riots"), and the expulsion of traditional republicans by the leadership during the 1960s.[^57]

At its October 1970 *Ard Fheis*, delegates were informed that an IRA convention had been held and had regularised its structure, bringing to an end the "provisional" period.[^58] By then, however, the label "Provisional" or "Provo" was already being applied to them by the media.[^59] The opposing, anti-abstentionist party became known as "Official Sinn Féin".[^60] It changed its name in 1977 to "Sinn Féin—The Workers' Party",[^51] and in 1982 to " [The Workers' Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers%27_Party_\(Ireland\) "Workers' Party (Ireland)") ".[^61]

Because the "Provisionals" were committed to military rather than political action, Sinn Féin's initial membership was largely confined, in [Danny Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Morrison_\(Irish_republican\) "Danny Morrison (Irish republican)") 's words, to men "over military age or women".[^62] A Sinn Féin organiser of the time in [Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast "Belfast") described the party's role as "agitation and publicity".[^62] New *cumainn* (branches) were established in Belfast, and a new newspaper, *[Republican News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_News "Republican News")*, was published.[^63] Sinn Féin took off as a protest movement after the introduction of [internment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Demetrius "Operation Demetrius") in August 1971, organising marches and pickets.[^64] The party launched its platform, *[Éire Nua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ire_Nua "Éire Nua")* ("a New Ireland") at the 1971 *Ard Fheis*.[^65] In general, however, the party lacked a distinct political philosophy. In the words of Brian Feeney, "Ó Brádaigh would use Sinn Féin *ard fheiseanna* (party conferences) to announce republican policy, which was, in effect, IRA policy, namely that Britain should leave the North or the 'war' would continue".[^66]

In May 1974, a few months after the [Sunningdale Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunningdale_Agreement "Sunningdale Agreement"), the ban on Sinn Féin was lifted by the UK [Secretary of State for Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Northern_Ireland "Secretary of State for Northern Ireland").[^49] Sinn Féin was given a concrete presence in the community when the [IRA declared a ceasefire in 1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army_actions_\(1970%E2%80%9379\)#1975 "Chronology of Provisional Irish Republican Army actions (1970–79)"). 'Incident centres', manned by Sinn Féin members, were set up to communicate potential confrontations to the British authorities.[^67]

From 1976, there was a broadcasting ban on Sinn Féin representatives in the Republic of Ireland, after the [Minister for Posts and Telegraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Posts_and_Telegraphs "Minister for Posts and Telegraphs"), [Conor Cruise O'Brien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conor_Cruise_O%27Brien "Conor Cruise O'Brien"), amended [Section 31 of the Broadcasting Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_31_of_the_Broadcasting_Act "Section 31 of the Broadcasting Act"). This prevented [RTÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89 "RTÉ") interviewing Sinn Féin spokespersons under any circumstances, even where the subject was not related to the Northern Ireland conflict.[^68] This lasted until 1994.

### 1976–1983

Political status for prisoners became an issue after the ending of the truce. Rees released the last of the internees, and ended ' [Special Category Status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Category_Status "Special Category Status") ' for all prisoners convicted after 1 March 1976. This led first to the [blanket protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanket_protest "Blanket protest"), and then to the [dirty protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirty_protest "Dirty protest").[^69] Around the same time, [Gerry Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Adams "Gerry Adams") began writing for *Republican News*, calling for Sinn Féin to become more involved politically.[^70] Over the next few years, Adams and those aligned with him would extend their influence throughout the republican movement and slowly marginalise Ó Brádaigh, part of a general trend of power in both Sinn Féin and the IRA shifting north.[^71] In particular, Ó Brádaigh's part in the 1975 IRA ceasefire had damaged his reputation in the eyes of northern republicans.[^72]

The prisoners' protest climaxed with the [1981 hunger strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Irish_hunger_strike "1981 Irish hunger strike"), during which striker [Bobby Sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Sands "Bobby Sands") was elected Member of Parliament for [Fermanagh and South Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermanagh_and_South_Tyrone_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\) "Fermanagh and South Tyrone (UK Parliament constituency)") as an [Anti H-Block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti_H-Block "Anti H-Block") candidate. After his death on hunger strike, his seat was held, with an increased vote, by his election agent, [Owen Carron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Carron "Owen Carron"). Two other Anti H-Block candidates were elected to [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann "Dáil Éireann") in the [general election in the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Irish_general_election "1981 Irish general election"). These successes convinced republicans that they should contest every election.[^73] Danny Morrison expressed the mood at the 1981 *Ard Fheis* when he said:

> Who here really believes we can win the war through the ballot box? But will anyone here object if, with a ballot paper in this hand and an [Armalite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArmaLite "ArmaLite") in the other, we take power in Ireland?[^74]

This was the origin of what became known as the [Armalite and ballot box strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armalite_and_ballot_box_strategy "Armalite and ballot box strategy"). Ó Brádaigh's chief policy, a plan for a federalised Irish state dubbed *[Éire Nua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ire_Nua "Éire Nua")*, was dropped in 1982, and the following year Ó Brádaigh stepped down as president, and was replaced by Adams.[^75]

### 1983–1998

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Gerry_Adams_and_Martin_McGuinness.jpg/250px-Gerry_Adams_and_Martin_McGuinness.jpg)

Under the political leadership of Gerry Adams and Martin McGuinness (pictured 2016), Sinn Féin adopted a reformist policy, eventually leading to the Good Friday Agreement.

Under Adams' leadership electoral politics became increasingly important. In 1983 [Alex Maskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Maskey "Alex Maskey") was elected to [Belfast City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_City_Council "Belfast City Council"), the first Sinn Féin member to sit on that body.[^76] Sinn Féin polled over 100,000 votes in the [Westminster elections that year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_United_Kingdom_general_election "1983 United Kingdom general election"), and Adams won the [West Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_West_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\) "Belfast West (UK Parliament constituency)") seat that had been held by the [Social Democratic and Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_and_Labour_Party "Social Democratic and Labour Party") (SDLP).[^76] [By 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1985_Northern_Ireland_local_elections#Overall "1985 Northern Ireland local elections") it had 59 seats on seventeen of the 26 Northern Ireland councils, including seven on Belfast City Council.[^77]

The party began a reappraisal of the policy of abstention from the Dáil. At the 1983 *Ard Fheis* the constitution was amended to remove the ban on the discussion of abstentionism to allow Sinn Féin to run a candidate in the forthcoming European elections. However, in his address, Adams said, "We are an abstentionist party. It is not my intention to advocate change in this situation." [^78] A motion to permit entry into the Dáil was allowed at the 1985 *Ard Fheis*, but did not have the active support of the leadership, and it failed narrowly.[^79] By October of the following year an IRA Convention had indicated its support for elected Sinn Féin TDs taking their seats. Thus, when the motion to end abstention was put to the *Ard Fheis* on 1 November 1986, it was clear that there would not be a split in the IRA as there had been in 1970.[^80] The motion was passed with a two-thirds majority. Ó Brádaigh and about twenty other delegates walked out, and met in a Dublin hotel with hundreds of supporters to re-organise as [Republican Sinn Féin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Sinn_F%C3%A9in "Republican Sinn Féin").[^81]

In October 1988, the British Conservative government followed the Republic in banning broadcasts of Sinn Féin representatives. Prime Minister [Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher "Margaret Thatcher") said it would "deny terrorists the oxygen of publicity". Broadcasters quickly found ways around the ban, mainly by using actors to dub the voices of banned speakers. The legislation did not apply during election campaigns and under certain other circumstances. The ban lasted until 1994.[^82]

Tentative negotiations between Sinn Féin and the British government led to more substantive discussions with the SDLP in the 1990s. Multi-party negotiations began in 1994 in Northern Ireland, without Sinn Féin. The Provisional IRA declared a ceasefire in August 1994. Sinn Féin then joined the talks, but the [Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\) "Conservative Party (UK)") government under [John Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Major "John Major") soon came to depend on unionist votes to remain in power. It suspended Sinn Féin from the talks, and began to insist that the IRA decommission all of their weapons before Sinn Féin be re-admitted to the talks; this led to the IRA calling off its ceasefire. The new [Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_\(UK\) "Labour Party (UK)") government of [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair "Tony Blair") was not reliant on unionist votes and re-admitted Sinn Féin, leading to another, permanent, ceasefire.[^83]

The talks led to the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement "Good Friday Agreement") of 10 April 1998, which set up an inclusive devolved government in Northern Ireland, and altered the Dublin government's constitutional claim to the whole island in [Articles 2 and 3 of the Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_2_and_3_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland "Articles 2 and 3 of the Constitution of Ireland"). Republicans opposed to the direction taken by Sinn Féin in the peace process formed the [32 County Sovereignty Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/32_County_Sovereignty_Movement "32 County Sovereignty Movement") in the late 1990s.[^84]

### 1998–2017

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Caoimhgh%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Caol%C3%A1in%2C_2019.jpg/250px-Caoimhgh%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Caol%C3%A1in%2C_2019.jpg)

The election of Caoimhghín Ó Caoláin to the Dáil in 1997 was the first time in 75 years a Sinn Féin TD had taken their seat and marked a turning point in the party's history

At the [1997 Irish general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Irish_general_election "1997 Irish general election"), [Caoimhghín Ó Caoláin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caoimhgh%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Caol%C3%A1in "Caoimhghín Ó Caoláin") was elected to the Dáil. In doing so, he became the first person under the "Sinn Féin" banner to be elected to Leinster House since [1957](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1957_Irish_general_election "1957 Irish general election"), and the first since 1922 to take their seat.[^85] [^86] [^87] Ó Caoláin's entry to the Dáil marked the beginning of a continuous Sinn Féin presence in the Republic of Ireland's national political bodies.

The party expelled [Denis Donaldson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Donaldson "Denis Donaldson"), a party official, in December 2005, with him stating publicly that he had been in the employ of the British government as an agent since the 1980s. Donaldson told reporters that the British security agencies who employed him were behind the collapse of the Assembly and set up Sinn Féin to take the blame for it, a claim disputed by the British government.[^88] Donaldson was found fatally shot in his home in [County Donegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Donegal "County Donegal") on 4 April 2006, and a murder inquiry was launched.[^89] In April 2009, the [Real IRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Irish_Republican_Army "Real Irish Republican Army") released a statement taking responsibility for the killing.[^90]

When Sinn Féin and the Democratic Unionist Party (DUP) became the largest parties, by the terms of the Good Friday Agreement no deal could be made without the support of both parties. They nearly reached a deal in November 2004, but the DUP insisted on photographic or video evidence that [decommissioning of IRA weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decommissioning_in_Northern_Ireland "Decommissioning in Northern Ireland") had been carried out, which was unacceptable to Sinn Féin.[^91]

In April 2006, a number of members of Sinn Féin who believed the party was not committed enough to socialism split from the party and formed a new group called [Éirígí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ir%C3%ADg%C3%AD "Éirígí"), which later became a (minor) political party in its own right.[^92]

On 2 September 2006, Martin McGuinness publicly stated that Sinn Féin would refuse to participate in a shadow assembly at Stormont, asserting that his party would only take part in negotiations that were aimed at restoring a power-sharing government. This development followed a decision on the part of members of Sinn Féin to refrain from participating in debates since the Assembly's recall the previous May. The relevant parties to these talks were given a deadline of 24 November 2006 to decide upon whether or not they would ultimately form the executive.[^93]

The 86-year Sinn Féin boycott of policing in Northern Ireland ended on 28 January 2007, when the *Ard Fheis* voted overwhelmingly to support the [Police Service of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Service_of_Northern_Ireland "Police Service of Northern Ireland") (PSNI).[^94] Sinn Féin members began to sit on Policing Boards and join District Policing Partnerships.[^95] There was opposition to this decision within Sinn Féin, and some members left, including elected representatives. The most well-known opponent was former IRA prisoner [Gerry McGeough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_McGeough "Gerry McGeough"), who stood in the [2007 Assembly election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Northern_Ireland_Assembly_election "2007 Northern Ireland Assembly election") against Sinn Féin in the constituency of [Fermanagh and South Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermanagh_and_South_Tyrone_\(Assembly_constituency\) "Fermanagh and South Tyrone (Assembly constituency)"), as an Independent Republican.[^96] He polled 1.8% of the vote.[^97] Others who opposed this development left to found the [Republican Network for Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Network_for_Unity "Republican Network for Unity").[^98]

Sinn Féin supported a no vote in the referendum on the [Twenty-eighth Amendment of the Constitution Bill 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-eighth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_Bill_2008 "Twenty-eighth Amendment of the Constitution Bill 2008").

Immediately after the [June 2017 UK general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election "2017 United Kingdom general election"), where the Conservatives won 49% of seats but not an overall majority, so that non-mainstream parties could have significant influence, Gerry Adams announced for Sinn Féin that their elected MPs would continue the policy of not swearing [allegiance to the Queen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_Allegiance_\(United_Kingdom\) "Oath of Allegiance (United Kingdom)"), as would be required for them to take their seats in the Westminster Parliament.[^99]

In 2017 and 2018, there were allegations of bullying within the party, leading to a number of resignations and expulsions of elected members.[^100]

At the *Ard Fheis* on 18 November 2017, Gerry Adams announced he would stand down as president of Sinn Féin in 2018, and would not stand for re-election as TD for [Louth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louth_\(D%C3%A1il_constituency\) "Louth (Dáil constituency)").

### 2018–present

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Mary_Lou_McDonald_and_Michelle_O%E2%80%99Neill_2018.jpg/250px-Mary_Lou_McDonald_and_Michelle_O%E2%80%99Neill_2018.jpg)

Mary Lou McDonald and Michelle O'Neill in February 2018

On 10 February 2018, [Mary Lou McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Lou_McDonald "Mary Lou McDonald") was announced as the new president of Sinn Féin at a special Ard Fheis in Dublin.[^101] [^102] [^103] [Michelle O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_O%27Neill "Michelle O'Neill") was also elected as vice president of the party.[^101]

Sinn Féin were opposed to Northern Ireland [leaving the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit "Brexit") together with the rest of the United Kingdom, with [Martin McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McGuinness "Martin McGuinness") suggesting a referendum on the [reunification of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Ireland "United Ireland") immediately after the [2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum "2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum") results were announced,[^104] a stance later reiterated by McDonald as a way of resolving the [border issues raised by Brexit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit_and_the_Irish_border "Brexit and the Irish border").[^105]

Sinn Féin's first elections under McDonald resulted in the party performing well under its own expectations during the [2018 Irish presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Irish_presidential_election "2018 Irish presidential election") that October,[^106] and similarly, the party's performance was labelled "disastrous" during the concurrent May [2019 European Parliament election in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_Parliament_election_in_Ireland "2019 European Parliament election in Ireland") and [2019 Irish local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Irish_local_elections "2019 Irish local elections"). In the European elections, Sinn Féin lost 2 MEPs and dropped their vote share by 7.8%, while in the local elections the party lost 78 (almost half) of their local councillors and dropped their vote share by 5.7%. McDonald stated "It was a really bad day out for us. But sometimes that happens in politics, and it's a test for you. I mean it's a test for me personally, obviously, as the leader".[^107]

However, in the [2020 Irish general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Irish_general_election "2020 Irish general election"), Sinn Féin received the greatest number of first preference votes nationally, making it the best result for any incarnation of Sinn Féin since [the 1922 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_Irish_general_election "1922 Irish general election").[^108] [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il "Fianna Fáil"), [Fine Gael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_Gael "Fine Gael") and the [Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Party_\(Ireland\) "Green Party (Ireland)") formed a coalition government in June 2020.[^109] Although second on seats won at the election, Sinn Féin became the largest party in the Dáil when [Marc MacSharry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_MacSharry "Marc MacSharry") resigned from [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il "Fianna Fáil") in September 2021, which, with [Seán Ó Fearghaíl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_%C3%93_Feargha%C3%ADl "Seán Ó Fearghaíl") sitting as [Ceann Comhairle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceann_Comhairle "Ceann Comhairle"), left Sinn Féin the largest party by one seat.[^110] Sinn Féin lost their numerical advantage in February 2022 following the resignation of [Violet-Anne Wynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet-Anne_Wynne "Violet-Anne Wynne").[^111]

In November 2020, the national chairman of Sinn Féin [Declan Kearney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declan_Kearney "Declan Kearney") contacted several dissident republican political parties such as [Saoradh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saoradh "Saoradh"), [Republican Network for Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Network_for_Unity "Republican Network for Unity") and the [Irish Republican Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Socialist_Party "Irish Republican Socialist Party") about creating a united republican campaign to call for a referendum on Irish unification. This information did not become publicly known until 2022 and the move was criticised in some quarters on the basis that it would be wrong for Sinn Féin to work with dissident republican groups which do not repudiate violence by paramilitaries. Sinn Féin retorted that engaging with dissident republicans draws them into the democratic process and political solutions instead of violent ones.[^112] [^113]

Sinn Féin won 29% of the [first-preference votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-preference_votes "First-preference votes") in the [2022 Northern Ireland Assembly election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Northern_Ireland_Assembly_election "2022 Northern Ireland Assembly election"), the highest share of any party. With 27 out of 90 seats, they became the largest party in Stormont for the first time ever.[^114] "Today ushers in a new era", O'Neill said shortly before the final results were announced. "Irrespective of religious, political or social backgrounds, my commitment is to make politics work." [^115]

Following the [2023 Northern Ireland local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Northern_Ireland_local_elections "2023 Northern Ireland local elections"), Sinn Féin became the largest party in local government for the first time.[^116] Then, in the local elections in the Republic of Ireland [in 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Irish_local_elections "2024 Irish local elections"), Sinn Féin increased their vote share, however, significantly fell short of the polls, showcasing a divide between the party's leadership and grassroots over immigration, with disgruntled Sinn Féin voters voting instead for small right-wing parties.[^117] [^118] However, following the [2024 United Kingdom general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election "2024 United Kingdom general election"), Sinn Féin became the single largest party representing Northern Ireland in Westminster.[^119]

## Past links with Republican paramilitaries

Sinn Féin is the largest [Irish republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_republican "Irish republican") political party, and was historically associated with the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1919%E2%80%931922\) "Irish Republican Army (1919–1922)"), while also having been associated with the [Provisional Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Irish_Republican_Army "Provisional Irish Republican Army") in the party's modern incarnation. The Irish government alleged that senior members of Sinn Féin have held posts on the [IRA Army Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRA_Army_Council "IRA Army Council").[^120] [^121] However, the SF leadership has denied these claims.[^122]

A republican document of the early 1980s stated: "Both Sinn Féin and the IRA play different but converging roles in the war of national liberation. The Irish Republican Army wages an armed campaign... Sinn Féin maintains the propaganda war and is the public and political voice of the movement".[^123] Robert White states at that time Sinn Féin was the junior partner in the relationship with the IRA, and they were separate organisations despite there being some overlapping membership.[^124]

Because of the party's links to the Provisional IRA, the [U.S. Department of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_State "United States Department of State") barred its members along with IRA volunteers from entering the U.S. since the early 1970s in accordance with the [Immigration and Nationality Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_and_Nationality_Act_of_1952 "Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952") on the grounds that they were associated with the IRA waging war against a legitimate government.[^125] [^126]

The [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_United_Kingdom "Government of the United Kingdom") stated in 2005 that "we had always said all the way through we believed that Sinn Féin and the IRA were inextricably linked and that had obvious implications at leadership level".[^127]

The [Northern Bank robbery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Bank_robbery "Northern Bank robbery") of £26.5 million in Belfast in December 2004 further delayed a political deal in Northern Ireland. The IRA were widely blamed for the robbery,[^128] although Sinn Féin denied this and stated that party officials had not known of the robbery nor sanctioned it.[^129] Because of the timing of the robbery, it is considered that the plans for the robbery must have been laid whilst Sinn Féin was engaged in talks about a possible peace settlement. This undermined confidence among [unionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionism_\(Ireland\) "Unionism (Ireland)") about the sincerity of republicans towards reaching agreement. In the aftermath of the row over the robbery, a further controversy erupted when, on [RTÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89 "RTÉ") 's *[Questions and Answers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questions_and_Answers_\(TV_series\) "Questions and Answers (TV series)")* programme, the chairman of Sinn Féin, [Mitchel McLaughlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchel_McLaughlin "Mitchel McLaughlin"), insisted that the IRA's controversial killing of a mother of ten young children, [Jean McConville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_McConville "Jean McConville"), in the early 1970s though "wrong", was not a crime, as it had taken place in the context of the political conflict. Politicians from the Republic, along with the Irish media, strongly attacked McLaughlin's comments.[^130] [^131]

On 10 February 2005, the government-appointed [Independent Monitoring Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Monitoring_Commission "Independent Monitoring Commission") reported that it firmly supported the [PSNI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Service_of_Northern_Ireland "Police Service of Northern Ireland") and [Garda Síochána](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garda_S%C3%ADoch%C3%A1na "Garda Síochána") assessments that the IRA was responsible for the Northern Bank robbery and that certain senior members of Sinn Féin were also senior members of the IRA and would have had knowledge of and given approval to the carrying out of the robbery.[^132] Sinn Féin has argued that the IMC is not independent, and that the inclusion of former [Alliance Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_Party_of_Northern_Ireland "Alliance Party of Northern Ireland") leader [John Alderdice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Alderdice "John Alderdice") and a British security head was proof of this.[^133] The IMC recommended further financial sanctions against Sinn Féin members of the Northern Ireland Assembly. The British government responded by saying it would ask MPs to vote to withdraw the parliamentary allowances of the four Sinn Féin MPs elected in 2001.[^134]

Gerry Adams responded to the IMC report by challenging the Irish government to have him arrested for IRA membership—a crime in both jurisdictions—and for [conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_\(crime\) "Conspiracy (crime)").[^135]

On 20 February 2005, the Irish [Minister for Justice, Equality and Law Reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Justice,_Home_Affairs_and_Migration "Minister for Justice, Home Affairs and Migration") [Michael McDowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_McDowell_\(politician\) "Michael McDowell (politician)") publicly accused three of the Sinn Féin leadership, Gerry Adams, [Martin McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McGuinness "Martin McGuinness") and [Martin Ferris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Ferris "Martin Ferris") (TD for [Kerry North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_North_\(D%C3%A1il_constituency\) "Kerry North (Dáil constituency)")) of being on the seven-man IRA Army Council; they later denied this.[^136] [^137]

On 27 February 2005, a demonstration against the murder of [Robert McCartney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_McCartney_\(murder_victim\) "Robert McCartney (murder victim)") on 30 January 2005 was held in east Belfast. [Alex Maskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Maskey "Alex Maskey"), a former Sinn Féin [Lord Mayor of Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Belfast "Lord Mayor of Belfast"), was told by relatives of McCartney to "hand over the 12" IRA members involved.[^138] The McCartney family, although formerly Sinn Féin voters themselves, urged witnesses to the crime to contact the PSNI.[^139] [^140] Three IRA men were expelled from the organisation, and a man was charged with McCartney's murder.[^141] [^142]

Irish [Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach "Taoiseach") [Bertie Ahern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertie_Ahern "Bertie Ahern") subsequently called Sinn Féin and the IRA "both sides of the same coin".[^143] In February 2005, [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann "Dáil Éireann") passed a motion condemning the party's alleged involvement in illegal activity. The [Bush Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_George_W._Bush "Presidency of George W. Bush") did not invite Sinn Féin or any other Northern Irish political party to the annual [St Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Day "St Patrick's Day") celebrations at the [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House "White House"), choosing instead to invite the family of Robert McCartney.[^144] Senator [Ted Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Kennedy "Ted Kennedy"), a regular sponsor of Gerry Adams' visits to the US during the peace process, also refused to meet Adams and hosted the McCartney family instead.[^144]

On 10 March 2005, the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom "House of Commons of the United Kingdom") in London passed without significant opposition a motion, introduced by the British government, to withdraw the allowances of the four Sinn Féin MPs for one year, in response to the Northern Bank Robbery. This measure cost the party approximately £400,000. However, the debate prior to the vote mainly surrounded the more recent events connected with the murder of Robert McCartney. Conservatives and unionists put down amendments to have the Sinn Féin MPs evicted from their offices at the House of Commons but these were defeated.[^145]

In March 2005, [Mitchell Reiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Reiss "Mitchell Reiss"), the [United States Special Envoy for Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Special_Envoy_for_Northern_Ireland "United States Special Envoy for Northern Ireland"), condemned the party's links to the IRA, saying "it is hard to understand how a European country in the year 2005 can have a private army associated with a political party".[^146]

The October 2015 [Assessment on Paramilitary Groups in Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assessment_on_Paramilitary_Groups_in_Northern_Ireland "Assessment on Paramilitary Groups in Northern Ireland") concluded that the Provisional IRA still existed "in a much reduced form", and that some IRA members believed its Army Council oversaw both the IRA and Sinn Féin, although it believed that the leadership "remains committed to the peace process and its aim of achieving a united Ireland by political means".[^147]
