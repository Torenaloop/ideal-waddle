The modern electric grid is built on a backbone of transformers. These giant machines, some as large as a shipping container and weighing as much as 800,000 pounds, allow utilities and grid operators to move electricity across the country at high voltages. They are also incredibly difficult to build and increasingly hard to find: Wait times for large power transformers — the kind needed to connect new power plants and gigawatt data centers to the grid — are as long as five years.

The first modern transformer built by William Stanley in 1886. Getty Images

Transformers are everywhere in our built environment, sitting atop telephone poles and hulking inside the substations across the United States. Some of them are beautiful. Some are so ugly they approach the sublime.

The basic design hasn’t changed in years: a core of steel with copper wound around it, using the interaction of magnetic fields and electric currents to increase or decrease voltages. Thomas Edison could look at a modern transformer and feel a flash of recognition. Making that design more efficient, and capable of handling today’s ultrahigh voltages (some transmission lines operate at 765,000 volts; your toaster is rated for 120), requires a combination of 19th-century craftsmanship and 21st-century materials science.

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/23/magazine/23mag-transformers-02/23mag-transformers-02-superJumbo.jpg)

To understand how this laborious process unfolds, the photographer Christopher Payne visited Delta Star, a transformer manufacturer based in Lynchburg, Va.

He watched as the company’s welders, machinists, winders, painters and other technicians assembled its products.

To keep the whole system from overheating, oil circulates between the cylindrical conservator at the top left, the core at the center and the cooling fins on the right.

The final price tag matches the intricacy of the production process: The largest power transformers can cost as much as $14 million each.

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/23/magazine/23mag-transformers-12/23mag-transformers-12-superJumbo-v2.jpg)

Some data-center companies are paying a premium to get their transformers first, making the demand crunch even worseand sending some customers, like rural electric cooperatives and solar farms, further back in line.

The grid will be at risk — slow to add new power sources, unable to respond to novel stresses and vulnerable to long-term blackouts — as long as the transformer shortage continues, and there’s no end in sight.
