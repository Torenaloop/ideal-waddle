Good eye — Stephen F. Austin was indeed born at Austinville, and that name is not a coincidence with the "Austinville" on your map. Let me check the record and verify the timing before answering. Here's the straight answer, separating what your research actually contains from what the geography suggests.

**In the corpus itself: no.** Nothing in your accumulated research — the working memory of the whole Nuckolls project — mentions the Austin family at all. There's no recorded marriage, deed, court interaction, or narrative tying a Nuckolls to an Austin. And a targeted web check turned up no documented Nuckolls–Austin link either. So as a _documented_ intertwining, the answer today is that none is on record.

**But the geography is genuinely striking — closer than you'd guess.** This isn't two families in the same county; it's two families on the _same tract_. The lead mines that made the Austins were known contemporaneously as "the mines on Cripple Creek" — on the south bank of the New River, at what became Austinville. Your own established research places Charles Nuckolls's **1780 entry of 1,000 acres "on New River and Cripple Creek"** at exactly that spot (the Ivanhoe/Austinville area, straddling the later Wythe/Grayson line). In other words, the "Austinville" pin on the map I built for you and Charles's original 1780 land are essentially the same ground.

The timing nearly overlaps too. Moses Austin (with his brother Stephen) secured the right to work the New River lead mines around **1790**; the settlement took the name Austinville about **1792**; and **Stephen F. Austin was born there November 3, 1793**, before the family left for Missouri around 1798. Per B.F. Nuckolls's _Pioneer Settlers_, Charles moved _from_ Cripple Creek _to_ Meadow Creek around **1790** — right as the Austins were arriving. So the two families may have been passing each other on the same land at the hinge of that decade.

One detail keeps a live Nuckolls presence there during the Austin years: the same book states that **Nathaniel Nuckolls owned part of that Cripple Creek land, lived there, and died in Wythe County.** So while Charles was relocating south, a Nuckolls remained a landowner in the very lead-mine district the Austins were developing.

**Verdict:** what you have is a compelling _proximity_ — same New River/Cripple Creek tract, same 1789–1798 window — but not a documented family connection. It's a strong lead, not a finding. That's exactly the kind of adjacency your notes flag as worth chasing in primary records rather than assuming.

If you want to test whether the proximity was ever an actual relationship, the records to pull are specific:

- **Wythe County Deed Books and Court Order Books, c.1790–1800** (Wythe formed 1790) — look for any Nuckolls⇄Austin land transfers, especially whether Charles's or Nathaniel's Cripple Creek acreage passed into the Austin mining operation, and any shared court appearances (Charles was a sitting Justice of the Peace in the adjacent new Grayson court by 1793).
- **Moses Austin's mine ledgers / labor and supply accounts** — local landowners often supplied or worked the mines; a Nuckolls name there would be a concrete link.
- **Montgomery County records pre-1790** for the original 1780 Nuckolls entry's exact boundaries relative to the mine tract.
