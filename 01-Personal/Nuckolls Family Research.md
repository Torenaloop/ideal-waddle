
# First Prompt

I'm interested in searching my family history through a great-great (or more) grandfather named Henry Carl Kalm Nuckolls whose children lived in Oklahoma. Specifically, from Henry's birth to as far back as records are reasonable.

# First Response

This is a fascinating family history with strong documentation going back to early Virginia and even to English origins. Here's what I've been able to piece together from multiple genealogical sources:

---

## The Nuckolls Family Line: Henry Carl Kalm Nuckolls and His Ancestors

---

### The Starting Point: Henry Carl Kalm Nuckolls

A quick note on the name: records show this individual as **Carl Henry Kalm Nuckolls**, born February 9, 1898, in Oskaloosa, Jefferson County, Kansas, and died April 16, 1968, in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. The "Henry" and "Carl" appear to have been used interchangeably in different records, and "Kalm" is a family middle name whose origin is explained below. He married Willie Mae Porter and later Maude Marilda Soard, and had sons who appear in Oklahoma records.

---

### Generation 1 (Parent): Franklin Savage Nuckolls

**Franklin Savage Nuckolls** was born August 28, 1871, in Hillsdale, Miami County, Kansas, and died August 16, 1943, in Henryetta, Okmulgee County, Oklahoma — which explains the family's Oklahoma connection. He was one of **fourteen children** of Charles Dabney Nuckolls and Elizabeth Bell Stevens. This large sibling group is important to understanding the "Kalm" middle name: one of Franklin's sisters was **Lena Julia Nuckolls**, born 1880, who married into the Kalm family. Carl Henry appears to have been named in honor of this maternal aunt's married name — a common 19th-century naming convention.

---

### Generation 2 (Grandparent): Charles Dabney Nuckolls

**Charles Dabney Nuckolls** was born approximately 1852 and died approximately 1935. He married **Elizabeth Bell Stevens** (born 1855, died 1910) in 1870. His fourteen children were born in Kansas between 1871 and 1900, spanning the family's frontier migration from Illinois to Kansas.

---

### Generation 3 (Great-Grandparent): Clark Nuckolls

**Clark Nuckolls** (born approximately 1811) married **Orlena Shellhouse** (born 1814, died 1854) in 1833, in Christian County, Illinois. Their six children were: Emily (1834), John Lewis (1836), Susan J. (1840), William (1848), Elizabeth (1850), and Charles Dabney (1852). Clark died in 1858, leaving his children orphaned as young adults. The family was established in the Sangamon County, Illinois area before the westward move to Kansas.

---

### Generation 4 (Great-Great-Grandparent): John Nuckolls & Ann Collins

**John Nuckolls** and **Ann Collins** were the parents of Clark (born c. 1811) and at least nine other children, including Matilda D. (1813), Martha (1819), James Dickenson (1824), Jane (1828), Amanda (1831), and Charles Samuel (1822).

Anna Collins was born 1782 (daughter of Mahlon Collins of the Quaker Collins family), and she married **John Nuckolls, born 1792**, in Grayson County, Virginia, on January 10, 1813. Grayson County was in the southwestern Blue Ridge mountains of Virginia — a significant family hub. The family later migrated to Sangamon County, Illinois, as part of the broader westward movement of Virginia families in the early 19th century.

---

### Generation 5 and Beyond: The Deeper Nuckolls Roots in Virginia

Here the documentation becomes both richer and more complex, as the Nuckolls name appears consistently across Grayson, Louisa, and surrounding Virginia counties.

Around 1790, a branch of the Nuckolls family came from Louisa County, Virginia, and settled on New River and Meadow Creek in the Grayson County area — which is exactly where John Nuckolls (born 1792) and Ann Collins were married. This strongly suggests John was part of this Louisa/Grayson County Nuckolls network.

The deeper Nuckolls genealogy is documented through _Pioneer Settlers of Grayson County, Virginia_ (Benjamin Floyd Nuckolls, 1914) and other sources. **John Nuckolls** (born July 12, 1755, died 1835) married **Mary Garland** in Louisa County, Virginia on July 12, 1777, and had ten children. He was a descendant of James Nuccol, who probably emigrated from England in the mid-1600s, and John performed Patriotic Service in Virginia during the American Revolution.

Going still further back, **James Nuckolls** was born June 30, 1670, in New Kent County, Virginia. He married Susannah Pouncey around 1694 in New Kent County. From him descended James Nuckolls II (born before 1695, died 1751 in Hanover County, VA), who married Elizabeth Duke around 1719, and then James Nuckolls III (born about 1720 in Hanover County, died March 17, 1810 in Louisa County), who married Mary Henderson.

Family tradition holds that the first Nuckolls brothers — **James, John, and William** — came from York, England in the early colonial era and were merchants at Jamestown, Virginia. This would place the family's American origins in the **mid-to-late 1600s**, making the Nuckolls an early colonial Virginia family with roots going back over 350 years on American soil.

## The Welsh Thread: What the Evidence Suggests

### 1. The Surname Itself Points to Wales (and the Celtic Fringe)

The name Nuccoll/Nuckolls is a phonetic corruption of the Nicol/Nichol/Nichols family of surnames, all derived from the given name Nicholas. Critically, the spelling variant "Nicholls" is specifically identified as a **Welsh surname** derived from the given name Nicholas, found predominantly in England and Wales, and "Nichols" is also of Welsh origin.

This is reinforced by historical distribution data: among the Nicholls/Nichols forms of the name, the great concentration was in Cornwall, Devon, Gloucestershire, and Wiltshire — and most tellingly, **Monmouthshire** (the Welsh border county) was "especially distinguished by its frequency." Monmouthshire was effectively the eastern gateway of Wales, and surnames concentrated there tend to have deep Welsh roots.

### 2. The Scottish Baptism Record — With a Celtic Twist

Here's where it gets interesting. A Scottish parish record identifies a **James Nuccoll** christened on October 11, 1629, in Inveresk with Musselburgh, Midlothian, Scotland, with father listed as **Williame Nuccoll**. This matches closely to the immigrant ancestor of the Virginia Nuckolls family.

The same Williame Nuccoll had another son, **Alexander Nuccol**, born August 12, 1621, in Musselburgh, Midlothian.

The geographic detail here is significant. Musselburgh/Inveresk sits just east of Edinburgh on the Firth of Forth. This was the heart of the old Scottish **Lothian** region — which, before the Anglo-Saxon and Norse expansions, was part of the **Brittonic Celtic kingdom of Gododdin**, a Welsh-speaking realm whose people were ethnically and linguistically Welsh. The famous early Welsh poem _Y Gododdin_ commemorates warriors from this very region. In other words, the Scottish Lowlands around Edinburgh were historically **Brythonic/Welsh** territory before they became "Scottish" in any modern sense.

### 3. The DNA Evidence Is Consistent

Y-DNA testing of a confirmed descendant of the founding James Nuccol places his haplogroup at **R-FT75336**, a subclade of R1b. The R1b1b2 haplogroup is most heavily concentrated in the British Isles and the northern seacoasts of Spain and France, and is thought to represent the progenitors of the Celts who migrated from Spain northward into France, Britain, and Ireland before written history. R1b — and especially its subclade R1b-L21 — is the dominant haplogroup of the Welsh, Irish, and Scots, and is sometimes called the "Celtic" marker. This is entirely consistent with a Welsh or Brittonic Celtic origin.

### 4. The Name in Scotland Was a Welsh Import

Scottish records from the 1600s show the Nuccol spelling appearing specifically in Lowland Scottish documents — "William Nuccol was tenant in the barony of Cannegleroch in 1640" and "William Nuckoll was a tailzeor [tailor] in Aberdeen in 1684." These are almost certainly related to the same family cluster. The Nuccol/Nuckoll spelling in Scotland, distinct from the more common Scottish "Nicol," looks like a family that carried a Welsh or Border variant of the name northward into the Scottish Lowlands, then emigrated to Virginia.

---

## Putting It Together: A Plausible Welsh Pathway

The most coherent picture the evidence suggests is:

1. The family's **Celtic origins** are real — the R1b DNA, the Welsh form of the surname (Nichols/Nicholls/Nuccoll), and the Monmouthshire concentration all point that direction.
2. At some point, likely in the **late medieval or early modern period** (1400s–1600s), a Welsh or Welsh-border family bearing a form of the Nicholas name migrated northward into the **Scottish Lowlands**, settling in Midlothian (around Musselburgh/Edinburgh).
3. James Nuccol, born approximately 1640–1650, emigrated from Scotland (or England) to colonial Virginia, arriving in New Kent County likely in the 1660s–1670s.
4. His son James was baptized in Virginia in 1670, and the Virginia Nuckolls line — your family's direct ancestors — descends from there.

The family tradition of Scottish origin isn't wrong, but Scotland may itself be a **waypoint** rather than the ultimate source. The deeper roots look Brythonic/Welsh.

---

## How to Pursue This Further

The most definitive next step would be **Y-DNA testing** of a living male Nuckolls descendant through FamilyTreeDNA. The Nuckolls Worldwide Kindred Society maintains an active DNA project specifically aimed at bridging the Atlantic and connecting American Nuckolls lines back to their British origins — they would be an invaluable resource. Their Big Y-700 test, in particular, could place your paternal line precisely on the haplotree and potentially identify matches with living Welsh families.

The documentary record essentially runs cold at **Williame Nuccoll of Musselburgh, Scotland** (father of James born 1629), but DNA could take the story much further back. Given the surname's Welsh character and the R1b haplogroup, the hypothesis of a Welsh origin migrating through Scotland to Virginia is not only plausible — it may well be exactly right.
